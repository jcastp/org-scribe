# Contemporary realism needs this file as much as fantasy does, and gets it
# wrong more often: the temptation is to skip it because "it's just a city".
# The section that matters is "How the world presses on the protagonist's
# Weakness". If a world does not press where the protagonist is weak, it is
# expensive scenery, and that is true of a gig economy exactly as much as of
# a magic system.

* The world in one line

A city that has quietly stopped buying people's time and started buying
their availability instead.

* Physical element developed

Distance and the twenty-five minutes. Everything in this book is a known
number of minutes from everything else, and the plot turns on one of them:
the agency lobby to Bell Lane is twenty-five minutes by car and there is no
version of the morning where both nine o'clock appointments happen. The
geography is developed exactly this far and no further, because this is the
only thing the plot asks of it.

* Cultural elements developed

- Work. Nobody in this book has an employer except Priya, and she has one
  she is not allowed to name that way. Payment is per project, invoiced at
  thirty days and cleared at ninety, so the actual unit of exchange is not
  money but the promise of being reachable next time.
- Care. The other institution running on the same shortage. Bell Lane has
  the hours and not the staff; Sam has the willingness and not the hours.
  The two economies never meet except through people who are in both, which
  in this book is Sam and, by the end, Alex.

* How the world presses on the protagonist's Weakness

Alex's Weakness is that they will not attach an hour to a commitment. A city
that rewards availability does not punish that — it *pays* for it. Alex's
defect is, in this specific economy, a marketable trait, and every actor in
the book who benefits from it says so out loud and means it kindly.

That is the pressure: the world does not oppose Alex's Weakness, it
subsidizes it. The only forces that require a stated hour are a day centre's
telephone and a dead woman's watch, and both are, by the standards of this
city, obsolete.

* Rules and limits

- An hour missed at Bell Lane is not rescheduled. It goes to the next name
  on the list. This costs a placement, and placements do not come back.
- A retainer at Okonkwo & Field is granted by one person and never
  negotiated. Refusing an hour is not against a rule; it is simply
  remembered.
- The watch is mechanical. Unwound for more than about thirty-six hours, it
  stops, and it does not tell you it has stopped. This is a rule with a cost
  and it is the reason the object is in the book.
