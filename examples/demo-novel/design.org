** Core

- Theme ::
  If you refuse to give your days a shape, you hand their shape to whoever
  asks for it first.

- Stances ::
  # Worst to best. Each one is carried by somebody: that is what turned this
  # list into a cast. See the Coverage table in the Characters section.
  1. Time is what other people take from you. The only defence is to promise
     nobody anything with an hour attached. (Alex, opening)
  2. Availability is loyalty. Whoever always answers deserves the work.
     (Dana)
  3. Keep your own hours by making sure you never owe anyone one. (Priya)
  4. A routine is a discipline you owe yourself, and keeping it makes you
     better than the people who don't. (Sam, opening)
  5. A shape you give the day is what lets you be reliably somewhere, for
     someone. (Sam, closing — and Alex, closing)

- Protagonist and their opening Stance ::
  Alex Rivera, on Stance 1.

- Ghost ::
  The morning Marisol died. Alex had promised to be at the hospice at nine.
  They lost the morning the way they lost every morning — three alarms, a
  shower that ran long, a client message answered on the stairs — and got
  there at eleven. She died at ten-forty.

- Lie ::
  Clocks are cruel, and looking at one is how you let it accuse you. Time
  cannot be held; planning it is a way of pretending otherwise, and the
  pretence is what makes the loss unbearable.

- Psychological Weakness ::
  Alex will not make any commitment with an hour attached to it. They say
  "sometime this week", "I'll swing by", "before the end of the day" — and
  mean all of it.

- Moral Weakness ::
  Alex spends Sam's mornings instead of their own. Concretely: Alex asks Sam
  to take the 8:00 courier handover for them, again, and that is the exact
  hour Sam has to be free to take the care centre's call about their father.

- Desire ::
  Win the twelve-month Kestrel retainer — the one contract that would end
  the scramble. It is pitched at nine in the morning, three weeks out.

- Need ::
  To give the day a shape, and keep one hour they promised.

- Opponent and their Stance ::
  Dana Okonkwo, on Stance 2. She wants the same thing Alex wants: control of
  Alex's mornings. Alex wants to own them; Dana needs to be able to spend
  them. Full chain in the Characters section.

- Stakes ::
  If Alex fails: the Kestrel retainer goes to someone else and the scramble
  becomes permanent; Sam's father loses the assessment slot and with it the
  day place; and Alex becomes, provably, a person who costs other people
  their mornings.

- Ending ::
  Rise, and does not get the Desire. See "The ending" below.

- Point of view ::
  Third person limited, past tense, single focalizer: Alex.

- Premise ::
  When a city pays for availability instead of time, a chronically
  unpunctual freelance designer must give their days a shape in order to
  keep the one promise that cannot be rescheduled.

** World Problem

# Expressed as an Aspect, so it can be invoked to complicate any scene.

- Aspect ::
  Nobody here is paid for their time; everyone is paid for their
  availability.

*** Box of complications

- A client message lands at 6:40 and is read before the kettle boils.
- A "quick call, ten minutes" is booked into the middle of the only
  uninterrupted block of the day.
- The freelance platform ranks by median response time, and the ranking is
  public.
- Rent clears on the 1st; invoices clear on the 30th, when they clear.

** The ending

- Moral movement ::
  Rise.
- Opening Stance → closing Stance ::
  1 → 5.
- External result ::
  Does not get the Desire. Alex misses the Kestrel pitch and loses the
  retainer to Priya.
- What that crossing proves about the Theme ::
  That the shape of a day is not a productivity technique. It is the only
  mechanism by which a promise to another person can be kept, and it costs
  something real, or it proves nothing.

** Designing principle and archetype

- Designing principle ::
  A person who has never looked at a clock learns to, one hour at a time,
  and the last hour he keeps is the one that costs him everything he was
  trying to buy with the others.
- Archetype ::
  The penitent.

** Point of view

- Person and tense ::
  Third person limited, past tense.
- Focalizers ::
  Alex only.
- Distance ::
  Close, but never inside the thought as it forms — Alex is a person who
  does not look directly at things, and the prose does not either.
- Narrative voice ::
  Dry, present-tense-adjacent, given to counting. Alex narrates in minutes.
- Rule for switching ::
  There is no switching. One focalizer.
- What this choice forbids me ::
  The reader can never watch Dana calculate — every one of her moves has to
  be legible from the outside, from what she says and when she sends it.
  And Priya's betrayal cannot be narrated: it has to be visible in the
  record, in a timestamp Alex can be shown.

*** Creative decisions

**** Point of view

- Narrator :: Third person limited, single focalizer (Alex).
- Tense :: Past.
- Consequence :: Priya's betrayal cannot be dramatized, only evidenced. This
  is a constraint the design took on deliberately; see design.org, "What this
  choice forbids me".

**** Tone

Dry and counting. Alex narrates in minutes, and the arithmetic disappears
from the prose in the last scene without comment. That disappearance is the
"Running late" thread closing (it carries =:Weight: -1= in the Plot part: tracked,
no column on the board).

**** Style

Short scenes. Enter late, leave early. The book is 80,000 words and no scene
is over 1,200.

** Symbols

# A symbol that does not change is decoration.

- Of the story ::
  Marisol's wristwatch. Mechanical: it stops unless it is wound, every day,
  by hand. The routine is not a metaphor for the watch; the watch is the
  routine.
- Of character ::
  Sam's paper notebook. Alex reads it as serenity for most of the book. It
  is a caregiver's log.
- Of change ::
  The watch stops at the apparent defeat, on the morning Sam misses the
  call. Alex winds it at the moral decision, and that is the whole
  self-revelation in one gesture.
- Of world ::
  The notification badge. A number that only ever goes up.
- Of action ::
  Making the bed. Absurd in Act I, sincere in Act III, and the same act.

** The Thirteen Non-Negotiables

*** 1. Theme Stated
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-01-theme-stated
:END:

Sam, in the coffee shop queue, Chapter 1 Scene 2: "I track it every day.
Sounds rigid, but it's actually quite freeing." Alex laughs and files it
under other people's hobbies. The theme is stated by someone who is not the
protagonist, and ignored.

*** 2. Weakness, Lie and Need
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-02-weakness-lie-need
:END:

The three alarms, Chapter 1 Scene 1 — the Weakness working. Then the 9:00
meeting Alex reaches on time and walks into without the presentation notes:
the Weakness costing. The Lie is visible in the watch, worn and never wound.

*** 3. Desire
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-03-desire
:END:

Dana names the Kestrel retainer over a phone call at 6:40 in the morning:
twelve months, one client, one scope. It starts weak — Alex assumes it is
not for them — and rises for three quarters.

*** 4. Inciting Incident
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-04-inciting-incident
:END:

The Halloran account is lost over an 8:00 courier handover Alex asked Sam to
cover and Sam, that once, could not. It happens *to* Alex, leaves them
distinctly worse off, and is the first time the cost lands on somebody else.

*** 5. Opponent
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-05-opponent
:END:

Dana states her Stance across a desk — "structure is a luxury; the rest of
us take the call" — and offers Alex more work on exactly the terms that
guarantee Alex can never be reliable. She wants the same eight hours Alex
wants, and she attacks the Weakness by praising it.

*** 6. Crossing the Threshold
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-06-crossing-threshold
:END:

Alex's own decision, alone on the couch: finds Marisol's watch under the
mail pile, fastens it on, writes "Day 1" at the top of a notebook and sets
an alarm for a stated hour. Closes the first quarter.

*** 7. Plan
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-07-plan
:END:

Copy Sam's routine exactly, to the minute, and be visibly reliable for three
weeks so that Dana has no argument left when the retainer is decided. It
fails, because it is a plan to impress the opponent rather than to change.

*** 8. Drive
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-08-drive
:END:

Three weeks of attempts, each a different kind: the imitation (Chapter 3),
the negotiation (Alex tries to buy the mornings back by working nights), and
the concealment (Alex starts lying about why they are unavailable). Each
loses, and each costs a little more of the moral ground.

*** 9. Midpoint
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-09-midpoint
:END:

False victory. Alex delivers twice on time and Dana praises them in front of
the studio — then books a 6:00 call "since you're up anyway". Alex refuses
it. First refusal in the book: they stop reacting and start acting. Closes
the second quarter.

*** 10. Apparent Defeat
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-10-apparent-defeat
:END:

Sam misses the Thursday call because Alex asked them to take the handover,
and the Bell Lane place goes to the next name on the list. In the same hour
Alex is forwarded a message chain with the timestamps left on, and sees that
Priya has been passing their free slots to Dana for a year. The watch has
stopped. Lowest point; closes the third quarter.

*** 11. Self-Revelation
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-11-self-revelation
:END:

- Glimpse (before the battle): Alex winds the watch. It is the first thing
  they have done at a stated hour because they said they would, and the Lie
  comes apart in the gesture: the clock did not take the morning Marisol
  died. Alex spent it.
- Confirmation (inside the battle): Alex says an hour out loud, to Dana, and
  keeps it.

*** 12. Battle
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-12-battle
:END:

The agency lobby at 8:40. The pitch is on the fourth floor at nine; the car
is at the kerb and Bell Lane is a twenty-five minute drive. Dana argues for
the two years she has never talked about, and argues well. Everything in the
book converges into one lobby and eleven minutes.

*** 13. Moral Decision
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: pp-13-moral-decision
:END:

Two goods, both with a cost: the Kestrel pitch, which would end the scramble
and let Alex pay Sam back in the only currency Alex has — or the drive to
the assessment, which is the hour Alex promised and the last one available.
Alex drives. Priya gets the retainer.

** Optional points used

- Opening image: an alarm silenced without either eye opening.
- Ghost: delivered late and in pieces, never as a flashback scene.
- Ally attack: Sam, at the apparent defeat — "you're not building a routine,
  you're building a way to feel better about the same mornings".
- Revelation to the audience: the reader sees the forwarded timestamps one
  scene before Alex reads them properly.
- Death of the Weakness: the watch is wound.
- New equilibrium: the coffee shop, six weeks later, with Casey.
- Final image: Alex looks at the watch on purpose, and has time.

** Quarters

- I Setup (0–25 %) ::
  Chapters 1–2. Closes on the crossing of the threshold: "Day 1", and an
  alarm set for a stated hour.
- II Reaction (25–50 %) ::
  Chapters 3–4. Closes on the midpoint: the refused 6:00 call.
- III Action (50–75 %) ::
  Chapters 5–6. Closes on the apparent defeat: the missed Thursday call and
  the timestamps.
- IV Resolution (75–100 %) ::
  Chapters 7–8. Closes on the moral decision and the new equilibrium.

*** Alex Rivera
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: char-alex-rivera-001
:Role: Protagonist
:Stance: Promise nobody an hour
:Goal: Win the Kestrel retainer
:Age: 28
:Occupation: Freelance graphic designer
:First-appearance: Chapter 1, Scene 1
:Weight: 1
:RelationshipsData: [[id:char-sam-chen-001][Sam Chen]]|neighbour; [[id:char-dana-okonkwo-001][Dana Okonkwo]]|client; [[id:char-priya-raman-001][Priya Raman]]|colleague; [[id:char-marisol-rivera-001][Marisol Rivera]]|family
:END:

- Concept :: A designer who has never once been where they said they would
  be, and has never once meant to let anyone down.
- Stance :: "The hours aren't mine. If I don't promise any of them, nobody
  can take one."
- Ghost :: The morning Marisol died. Alex promised nine. Three alarms, a
  long shower, a client message answered on the stairs. Eleven. She died at
  ten-forty.
- Lie :: Clocks are cruel, and looking at one is how you let it accuse you.
  Time cannot be held; planning it only makes losing it worse.
- Trouble :: Rent clears on the 1st. Two invoices are ninety days out and
  the studio that owes them has stopped answering.
- Psychological Weakness :: Will not attach an hour to any commitment.
  "Sometime this week." "I'll swing by." "Before end of day."
- Moral Weakness :: Spends Sam's mornings instead of their own. Asks Sam to
  take the 8:00 courier handover — the exact hour Sam has to be free for the
  care centre's call.

**** Desire

- Goal :: Win the twelve-month Kestrel retainer. Pitch is at nine, three
  weeks out.
- Motivation :: That once the money is steady, the days will hold
  themselves. (Out of the Lie, so false underneath: it puts the shape of the
  day in the money's gift rather than Alex's.)
- Conflict :: Dana, who decides who gets the retainer and needs Alex
  available rather than booked.

**** Need

- Goal :: Give the day a shape and keep one hour they promised.
- Motivation :: To be someone who can be counted on at a named hour — which
  is exactly what Sam has stopped counting on.
- Conflict :: The Lie, and what Alex would have to admit about the morning
  Marisol died: that the clock did not take that morning. Alex did.

**** Plan

- Plan :: Copy Sam's routine exactly, to the minute, and be visibly reliable
  for three weeks so that Dana has no argument left.
- Fallback plan :: Take the 6:00 calls. Be so available that the retainer is
  cheaper to grant than to refuse.

**** Aspects

- Aspect :: "I do my best work at two in the morning." True, and it is the
  sentence Dana uses to buy them.
- Aspect :: Cannot stand to watch someone else be late.
- Aspect :: Marisol's watch on their wrist, unwound.
- Relationship :: Sam from 4B has a key to Alex's flat, and Alex has never
  once had to ask twice.
- Possession :: The watch. Mechanical, and it stops unless it is wound by
  hand every day.

**** Backstory

Alex is nineteen, at the kitchen table in Marisol's flat, and has been there
since seven because the bus times only work that way. Marisol comes in at
eight, makes tea for two without asking, and they sit. At ten to nine she
puts her cup down, says "I have the doctor at half past", stands, and goes.
Alex remembers the hour more clearly than anything she said, and has never
understood why the memory feels like an accusation.

**** Interrogation

1. What would they never do?
   Say a time out loud and then not be there. It is the only promise Alex
   treats as unbreakable, which is why they have arranged a life in which
   they never make one.
2. What do they do when they are alone and nobody is watching?
   Count. Minutes to the door, minutes to the meeting, minutes left. Alex
   narrates their whole life in arithmetic and has never once written any of
   it down.
3. What are they wrong about?
   That the morning Marisol died was taken from them. It was spent.
4. What would they die for?
   Not being the reason someone else missed something.
5. Write them a trivial scene.
   Ordering coffee, Alex says "whatever's fastest", and then waits four
   minutes for the fastest thing while a filter coffee sits ready on the
   counter.

** Worldbuilding

#+begin_comment
A worked example. The world, not the places — specific settings with their
Echo and Hazard are in the Setting section.

Contemporary realism needs this file as much as fantasy does, and gets it
wrong more often: the temptation is to skip it because "it's just a city".
The section that matters is "How the world presses on the protagonist's
Weakness". If a world does not press where the protagonist is weak, it is
expensive scenery, and that is true of a gig economy exactly as much as of
a magic system.
#+end_comment

*** The world in one line

A city that has quietly stopped buying people's time and started buying
their availability instead.

*** Physical element developed

Distance and the twenty-five minutes. Everything in this book is a known
number of minutes from everything else, and the plot turns on one of them:
the agency lobby to Bell Lane is twenty-five minutes by car and there is no
version of the morning where both nine o'clock appointments happen. The
geography is developed exactly this far and no further, because this is the
only thing the plot asks of it.

*** Cultural elements developed

- Work. Nobody in this book has an employer except Priya, and she has one
  she is not allowed to name that way. Payment is per project, invoiced at
  thirty days and cleared at ninety, so the actual unit of exchange is not
  money but the promise of being reachable next time.
- Care. The other institution running on the same shortage. Bell Lane has
  the hours and not the staff; Sam has the willingness and not the hours.
  The two economies never meet except through people who are in both, which
  in this book is Sam and, by the end, Alex.

*** How the world presses on the protagonist's Weakness

Alex's Weakness is that they will not attach an hour to a commitment. A city
that rewards availability does not punish that — it *pays* for it. Alex's
defect is, in this specific economy, a marketable trait, and every actor in
the book who benefits from it says so out loud and means it kindly.

That is the pressure: the world does not oppose Alex's Weakness, it
subsidizes it. The only forces that require a stated hour are a day centre's
telephone and a dead woman's watch, and both are, by the standards of this
city, obsolete.

*** Rules and limits

- An hour missed at Bell Lane is not rescheduled. It goes to the next name
  on the list. This costs a placement, and placements do not come back.
- A retainer at Okonkwo & Field is granted by one person and never
  negotiated. Refusing an hour is not against a rule; it is simply
  remembered.
- The watch is mechanical. Unwound for more than about thirty-six hours, it
  stops, and it does not tell you it has stopped. This is a rule with a cost
  and it is the reason the object is in the book.

Contemporary realism, so the rules are social rather than physical, and they
live in the Worldbuilding section. The three that constrain the plot:

- An hour missed at Bell Lane goes to the next name on the list.
- A retainer at Okonkwo & Field is granted by one person and never
  negotiated.
- The watch runs about thirty-six hours and does not warn you.

*** Marisol's wristwatch
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: obj-wristwatch-001
:Type: Symbol of the story / Symbol of change
:Owner: Alex Rivera (inherited)
:First-appearance: Chapter 2, Scene 1
:Status: Stopped for the first two-thirds of the book
:END:

**** Physical Description

A 1960s mechanical wristwatch on a worn leather strap, small by modern
standards, with a face that has yellowed evenly. No battery, no alarm, no
date. It runs for about thirty-six hours on a full wind and gives no warning
before it stops.

**** Origin

Marisol Rivera wore it every day for fifty years. It has been under the mail
pile on Alex's coffee table for two of them.

**** Importance in the Plot

It is the routine, not a metaphor for it. A mechanical watch has to be wound
by hand, at roughly the same time, every day, or it silently stops being
right. Everything the book argues about days is already true of this object.

**** Object History

- Chapter 2, Scene 1 :: Found under the mail. Alex puts it on and does not
  wind it.
- Chapters 3–5 :: Worn daily, never wound. It is right twice a day and Alex
  never notices, because Alex does not check.
- Chapter 6, Scene 2 :: Alex looks at it properly for the first time. It
  reads 8:00 at twenty to eleven at night.
- Chapter 7, Scene 1 :: Wound. Forty seconds, badly, and it is the whole
  self-revelation.
- Chapter 8, Scene 2 :: Wound every morning, unremarked.

**** Symbolism

Of the story, and of change. The two are the same object on purpose: the
thing that stands for the theme is also the thing that visibly alters.

**** Rules and limitations

Thirty-six hours. It does not announce that it has stopped. See
the Worldbuilding section, "Rules and limits" — a rule with no cost is not a
rule, and this one costs Alex the moment they finally read the face.

**** Notes

Two continuity errors are currently in the draft: Chapter 2 Scene 1 says the
watch is "still ticking", and Chapter 3 Scene 1 gives it an alarm. Both are
flagged with *EDIT*: plot markers in novel.org and will show up in the edit
index (=F8 6=).

***** Description

Bell Lane calls at eight on Thursdays to confirm the following week's

***** Consequences

Two consecutive misses drop the name to the bottom of the list. Sam misses

out on a reread.

***** Connections

This is the immovable hour the whole book is arranged around. Alex spends it
three times: Chapter 2 Scene 2 (refused), Chapter 6 Scene 1 (granted, and
fatal), and Chapter 7 Scene 1 (given back, with a number attached).

***** Notes

The five-minute duration matters. It is a *small* hour, and that is the
point: what Alex has been taking is never large.

*** Adult day-centre assessment and waiting lists
:PROPERTIES:
:CATEGORY: Care
:PRIORITY: High
:STATUS: Answered — needed for Ch. 6 and Ch. 8
:END:

**** Questions to Answer

- How is a place allocated, and what actually happens when contact is missed?
- Is there a real in-person assessment, and can it be rescheduled?
- What is the plausible interval between the two?

**** Sources

- Local authority adult social care pages (provisional placement procedure).
- One conversation with a day-centre manager, notes not for quotation.

**** Key Findings

- Provisional places are held against periodic contact; sustained silence
  moves the name down rather than off.
- An in-person assessment is real and is the gate. Rescheduling is possible
  once, and the second offer is not negotiable on time.
- Two to three weeks between the two is normal.

**** How This Applies to Story

This is the entire mechanism of the last quarter. The plot needs an
appointment that (a) cannot move, (b) was rescheduled because of something
Alex did, and (c) collides with the pitch by accident rather than by Dana's
design. All three are true of the real procedure, which is why the collision
does not feel arranged.

*** Mechanical watch behaviour
:PROPERTIES:
:CATEGORY: Objects
:PRIORITY: Medium
:STATUS: Open — needed before Ch. 6 Scene 2
:END:

**** Questions to Answer

- How long does a 1960s manual-wind movement run on a full wind?
- What does it do as it runs down — does it slow before it stops?
- Would it still run at all after two years in a drawer?

**** Sources

- To do: one watchmaker, one afternoon.

**** Key Findings

Provisional: roughly thirty-six to forty hours, and it loses time before it
stops rather than stopping cleanly. If that is right, Alex could be walking
around with a watch that is quietly wrong for hours, which is better than the
current draft and should go in.

**** How This Applies to Story

even the provisional answer twice (see the *EDIT*: plot markers in novel.org),
