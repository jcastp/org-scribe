* Welcome to Lesson 18!

Lesson 1 mentioned, in one paragraph, that project creation asks *which
plotting method* to use. This lesson is that paragraph expanded: what the
three methods are, when each one earns its keep, what their five levels
actually ask you to do, and — the part that matters most if you already have
a project — exactly what changes and what does not when you pick one.

By the end of this lesson, you'll know how to:
- Decide which of the three methods fits the story you're about to write
- Read and fill in La Hélice's five levels, in order
- Read and fill in La Matriz's five levels, in order
- Recognize the one thing both new methods deliberately do *not* automate,
  and why
- Confirm that the health report (~F8 F8 H~) checks your Starting Gate the
  same way no matter which method you picked
- Find the troubleshooting note built into each design file for when a
  level's check keeps failing

*Prerequisites*: Lesson 1 (you need to have created a project, or be about
to). Nothing else in this guide depends on which method you choose — every
other lesson works identically under all three.

*Estimated time*: 25 minutes to read, longer to actually use one.

* Part 1: Three Methods, One Vocabulary

Every method available at project creation is built on the same underlying
vocabulary: a causal chain per character (Ghost → Lie → Weakness →
Desire/Need), the thirteen non-negotiable plot points, and the four quarters
that structure your novel. They differ only in *what order you fill things in*
and *where the plot comes from*.

| Method             | You start from…               | The plot comes from…                                                  |
|--------------------+-------------------------------+-----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| *Sistema unificado*  | A theme and a Stance spectrum | Escalating trama and hanging characters from it                       |
| *La Hélice* (Helix)  | A premise                     | Characters scaled to their own five levels, in parallel with the plot |
| *La Matriz* (Matrix) | Characters                    | Deducing collisions from characters who cannot avoid each other       |

*Sistema unificado* is the default and the one every other lesson in this
guide assumes — it's what the demo novel (~examples/demo-novel/~) uses. This
lesson is about the other two.

** What Actually Changes

This is worth stating plainly, because it's easy to assume a "method" changes
your whole project:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Choosing La Hélice or La Matriz changes *one file*: ~design.org~
(~diseno.org~ in Spanish projects). Everything else — ~novel.org~, ~notes/~,
and every file under ~objects/~ — is identical to a Sistema project's.
#+END_QUOTE

That's not an accident or a simplification for this guide — it follows
directly from the shared vocabulary above. Both new methods import the
Sistema's character profile, its thirteen non-negotiables and its four
quarters rather than replacing them, so nothing downstream of the design
file — capture, linking, the health report, compilation, the planner — needs
to know or care which method you picked. Lesson 4 (character capture),
Lesson 6 (plot threads), Lesson 11 (export) and every other lesson in this
guide work exactly the same regardless.

* Part 2: Choosing at Project Creation

The method prompt appears as Step 7 of novel project creation (see Lesson 1):

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Plotting method: Sistema unificado (default)
#+END_EXAMPLE

Press ~Tab~ to see all three, or ~Enter~ to accept the Sistema default. Your
choice is written to ~.org-scribe-project~ as a ~# Method:~ line — the same
mechanism that records your project's language — so it's a one-time decision
remembered for that project from then on.

*This prompt only appears for novel projects.* Short story projects don't
offer a method choice; they keep their existing minimal structure.

If you're not sure which to pick, read the "When to use it" subsection under
each method below, or just start with Sistema unificado — you lose nothing
by trying the other two later on a fresh project, since the choice is made
once, at creation, per project.

* Part 3: La Hélice ("The Helix")

** When to Use It

Use La Hélice when *you already have a premise* — a sentence like "a pirate
who's spent twenty years never stopping must hold still to destroy the
magistrate who burned his village" — and the characters who would live that
premise haven't taken shape yet.

** The Three Rules

Before the levels, three rules that make this a method rather than a list of
fields — they're printed at the top of the design file so you always have
them nearby:

- *Rule of scaling*: don't move to the next level until both of the current
  level's documents (one for plot, one for character) are written and their
  check passes. Never write one three levels ahead of the other.
- *Rule of ownership*: every disaster has an owner — a specific person and a
  specific Lie that causes it. If a disaster has no owner, the disaster is
  badly designed, not missing a character.
- *Rule of return*: when a check fails, rewrite at the level where it failed,
  not the one below it, and don't keep descending until it passes.

** The Five Levels

Each level (except the last) produces *two sibling documents* — one about
plot, one about character, at the same level of detail — plus a check that
crosses them.

| Level | Plot side                             | Character side                                        |
|-------+---------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------|
|     1 | The ~15-word novel sentence           | The protagonist's Lie and Desire only                 |
|     2 | Setup + 3 disasters + external ending | Full chain, protagonist then opponent                 |
|     3 | One-page synopsis by quarters, Theme and Stances named | One page per force, with change milestones            |
|     4 | Four-page synopsis                    | Full profile per force                                |
|     5 | Scene list                            | (folded into the scene list's PoV/motivation columns) |

*Level 2 is the decisive one* — budget more time there than anywhere else. It
produces the three disasters (see Part 5 below), each with an owner, and the
cross-check of the two endings: what the story looks like from the outside
(does the protagonist get the Objective?) and from the inside (do they let go
of the Lie?), written without looking at each other and then crossed in a
grid.

The design file calls out two traps right where they bite: a disaster that
nobody causes — the storm, the plague, the royal edict — can sit in the
background but never counts as a structural disaster; and the Motivation has
to sound like the *promise* the Lie makes, so if it reads as sincere, one of
the two fields is wrong. It also spells out, under the Opponent heading, the
three checks that most often get skipped: they want the same thing as the
protagonist (not just the same object — the same thing underneath it), their
Stance can be argued with real conviction, and ideally their Ghost is the
protagonist's own Ghost with the opposite conclusion drawn from it.

*Level 3 is also where your Theme gets named* — unlike the Sistema, which
names it before any character exists, La Hélice deduces it late, from what
you already have: the protagonist's Lie, the opponent's Stance, and the
two-endings cross-check. Name it here, then build the Stance list by
reading off the positions your living forces already hold rather than
building the list first and casting to fill it. If you captured a
character's ~:Stance:~ earlier (level 2), that value was necessarily
provisional — check it against the list now that it exists.

** The Exit Gate

You can start drafting once *level 3 is closed and its check passes*, plus
your first three scenes are written. Levels 4 and 5 are there for when you
need them or the draft stalls — not a requirement to start.

* Part 4: La Matriz ("The Matrix")

** When to Use It

Use La Matriz when *what you have are characters* and the story hasn't
arrived yet — you know two to four people well, but not what happens between
them. It's also the better fit for a coral story with three or four
comparably weighted forces, where La Hélice would tend to sort everyone into
a single protagonist/opponent pair.

Two situations where it's the wrong tool: if you're starting from a premise
or a commissioned pitch (use La Hélice instead — inverting the method throws
away what you already have), or if you're working in a genre with strong
plot expectations — a mechanism thriller, a puzzle mystery — where the
machinery has to lead and the arc follows. (A one-paragraph version of this
"weakest fit" note ships right in the design file too, under the method's own
heading — you don't need to come back to this lesson to be reminded.)

** The Three Rules

- *Rule of the arena*: there's no deduction without scarcity. Two characters
  with incompatible Lies but nothing to fight over don't collide — they just
  ignore each other. That's why level 1 asks for an object in dispute, and a
  place and moment where everyone has to be at once.
- *Rule of inevitability*: among every possible collision, pick the one
  neither force can dodge without ceasing to be who they are. This replaces
  authorial taste as the selection criterion.
- *Rule of the late hook*: the sellable fifteen-word sentence still gets
  written — just at the *end* of level 3 instead of the beginning, and it's a
  gate: if it doesn't come out, go back to level 1.

** The Five Levels

The dependency order is *inverted* relative to La Hélice: the first two
levels are about character, and the plot is born in the third.

| Level | What you produce                                                                         |
|-------+------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
|     1 | The census (2–4 one-line force statements) and the arena (object, place/moment, clock)   |
|     2 | One full chain per force: Ghost → Lie → Weakness → Trouble → Desire → Need               |
|     3 | The collision matrix, the collisions you choose from it, the skeleton, and the hook test |
|     4 | One-page synopsis by quarters, Theme and Stances named, with change milestones          |
|     5 | Scene list                                                                               |

*Level 3 is the method's signature move.* You fill an N×N matrix — one row
and column per force — where cell (A, B) says what A's Lie does to B's Lie
when both go after the same object. A filled cell should read like a
sentence, not a word — the design file's own example: not "conflict" but
"his signature from years ago is the reason her home doesn't exist anymore."
From the candidates in that matrix, you pick your collisions by
inevitability, and only *then* — after the plot has been deduced — do you
write the fifteen-word hook. If it doesn't come out, that's not a level-3
problem to push through: it's a sign the level-1 census was wrong (missing a
force, missing a clock), and you go back there.

The census itself comes with a worked example of the pattern it rewards:
two characters who each lost something to an authority as children, one
concluding "keep moving, never stop" and the other "get on the side that
signs the papers" — same wound, opposite lesson, and they collide without
either of them trying to.

*Level 4 is also where your Theme gets named* — and here it comes out on
its own: deducing the plot from incompatible Lies produces the Theme
without ever declaring it first. Naming it at this level is putting words
to what the collision matrix has already been arguing about since level 3.
Build the Stance list the same way — take each force's Stance from its
Lie — rather than casting characters to fill a list decided in advance.

** The Exit Gate

The gate sits *one level later* than La Hélice's — at the close of level 4,
not level 3. That's deliberate: closing level 3 here gives you a skeleton
that was just deduced from the matrix but hasn't yet been tested against a
synopsis's actual pace. Level 4 is where this method checks that what it
deduced actually holds up as narrative.

* Part 5: Where the Plot's Weight Lives

Both methods make the same three-or-so structural moments — La Hélice's
*disasters*, La Matriz's *collisions* — visible in a table near the end of
the design file:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
| Disaster | Owner | Lie that causes it | Explodes on whom | Position           |
|----------+-------+---------------------+-------------------+---------------------|
|        1 |       |                     |                   | End of quarter I    |
|        2 |       |                     |                   | Midpoint            |
|        3 |       |                     |                   | End of quarter III  |
#+END_EXAMPLE

This table is deliberately *just a table* — plain prose in ~design.org~, not
a linked entity the way characters, locations and plot threads are. Nothing
in org-scribe checks automatically that every force owns one, or that every
disaster has a scene. It's there so that, at a glance, you know where your
story's weight falls before you translate that into actual scenes — a
working note, exactly like the Sistema's own Stakes table, not a fourth or
fifth thing the linking system tracks.

If you want the ownership and coverage checks a linked entity would give you
automatically, that's a reasonable thing to want — it just isn't what this
version does. Track it by eye for now: three rows is not a lot to watch.

* Part 6: When a Check Keeps Failing

Both design files end with a short section, right before ~Pending design
changes~, for exactly the moment this lesson can't help you with directly —
you're stuck, a check won't pass, and you don't know which level to go back
to. It reads a symptom and names the level:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
* Where to look when something's wrong

# "Things happen but mean nothing" → level 2, rule of ownership.
# "The opponent feels like cardboard" → level 2: undefendable Stance, or
#   wants a different thing than the protagonist.
# "The ending doesn't convince me" → level 2, the two-endings cross-check.
# "Nothing changes until the very end" → level 3: missing Adjustments
#   between the Breaks.
#+END_EXAMPLE

That's La Hélice's version. La Matriz's covers its own five symptoms instead
— "everyone ignores everyone" points at level 1's rule of the arena, "I keep
choosing the collision myself" points at level 3's rule of inevitability, and
so on — because the two methods fail in different places: La Hélice's
weak point is usually the opponent or the ending cross-check (both level 2);
La Matriz's is usually the arena or the matrix itself (levels 1 and 3).

It's adapted from each method's own source material — the "Diagnóstico
rápido" table in ~copo-helice.org~ and the "Riesgos y sus mitigaciones" table
in ~copo-matriz.org~ — condensed to the handful of symptoms you're most
likely to actually hit. Read it before you assume a check is wrong; it's
usually pointing at the level above.

* Part 7: The Starting Gate Still Works the Same Way

Whichever method you picked, ~F8 F8 H~ (the project health report) opens
with your *Starting Gate* section exactly as it does for a Sistema project —
your own checkboxes read back, plus two measured items reported alongside
them regardless of the method or the language: how many of the thirteen
plot points actually have content, and how many of your first three scenes
have a nonzero word count.

The only difference is *where* the gate sits and what it asks:

| Method  | Gate closes at…                           |
|---------+-------------------------------------------|
| Sistema | Eight items, no level structure           |
| Hélice  | End of level 3, plus three scenes written |
| Matriz  | End of level 4, plus three scenes written |

You don't need to do anything differently to get this — the health report
finds your Starting Gate section and its two measured items by what they
say, not by counting checkboxes in a fixed order, so it works the same way
under all three methods.

* Try It Now

1. Create a second, throwaway project (~M-x org-scribe-create-novel-project~)
   and pick a different method than your main project uses.
2. Open its ~design.org~ (or ~diseno.org~) and compare it side by side with
   your main project's.
3. Confirm for yourself that ~novel.org~ and everything under ~objects/~ look
   the same in both — copy one of your own characters' profiles into the new
   project's ~objects/characters.org~ and see that nothing about it needs to
   change.
4. Scroll to the bottom of the new ~design.org~/~diseno.org~ and read its
   "Where to look when something's wrong" section — it's a useful reference
   to bookmark once you're actually working through the levels.
5. Delete the throwaway project once you're done; nothing about it is worth
   keeping unless you decide to actually write that story.
