* Welcome to Lesson 6!

In this lesson, you'll learn how to track multiple plot threads throughout your story. Whether you're writing a single-storyline short story or a complex novel with multiple interweaving plots, the plot tracking system helps you ensure balanced development and avoid forgotten storylines.

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to:
- Create plot thread profiles for main and subplots
- Understand plot properties and how to use them
- Link plot threads to scenes using ID-based links
- Search for all scenes that develop a specific plot thread
- Generate plot timeline visualizations
- Check plot health to find gaps and imbalances
- Ensure all plot threads reach satisfying conclusions

*Estimated time*: 20 minutes

* Why Track Plot Threads?

**Quick answer**: Plot tracking prevents dropped storylines, ensures balanced development, and helps you see the big picture.

** The Problem Without Plot Tracking

When writing without plot thread tracking, you might:
- Introduce a subplot in Chapter 2, then forget about it until Chapter 15
- Realize your main plot only develops in 40% of scenes
- Have a romance subplot that appears three times then vanishes
- Struggle to remember which scenes advance which plots
- Write 10 consecutive scenes all focused on the same plot (exhausting!)
- Finish drafting and discover a major plot thread was never resolved

** The Solution: Plot Thread Profiles

Plot thread profiles help you:
1. **Track Development**: See exactly where each plot advances
2. **Balance Storylines**: Ensure all plots get appropriate attention
3. **Find Gaps**: Identify long stretches where a plot disappears
4. **Plan Resolution**: Track which plots are resolved vs. ongoing
5. **Visualize Structure**: See how plots interweave across your story
6. **Prevent Forgetting**: Never lose track of a subplot again

* The Thirteen Non-Negotiables

Before anything about threads, there is a second thing in ~objects/plot.org~,
and it comes first in the file for a reason.

** What They Are

Thirteen plot points that ship with every novel project, already written into
the template, waiting for you to fill in their bodies:

| #  | Plot point             | #   | Plot point          |
|----+------------------------+-----+---------------------|
|  1 | Theme Stated           |   8 | Drive               |
|  2 | Weakness, Lie and Need |   9 | Midpoint            |
|  3 | Desire                 |  10 | Apparent Defeat     |
|  4 | Inciting Incident      |  11 | Self-Revelation     |
|  5 | Opponent               |  12 | Battle              |
|  6 | Crossing the Threshold |  13 | Moral Decision      |
|  7 | Plan                   |     |                     |

They are called non-negotiable because with them the story is complete and
causal, and without one of them it is not. Filling them in is box 7 of the
Starting Gate (Lesson 1) — the template's advice is to start here and look at
nothing else until you have all thirteen.

*You never create or delete them.* Unlike characters, settings and threads,
this is a fixed list, identical in every project, and there is no capture
command for it. You write into the thirteen headings that are already there.

** Tagging a Scene With Its Plot Point

Once a scene serves one of the thirteen, tag it:

: F8 F8 G     (org-scribe-set-scene-plot-points)

Pick from the list; the scene's ~:Plot-point:~ property gets an ID link, the
same mechanism as every other entity. To go the other way — from a scene to
the plot point it serves — use:

: F8 F8 L J   (org-scribe-jump-to-plot-point)

If your project predates the linking setup, ~F8 F8 L P~ mints the IDs once.

** Why This Matters More Than It Looks

The project health report (~F8 F8 H~) lists *every one of the thirteen that no
scene serves yet*. That single list is the method's own completeness check,
mechanized: a plot point nothing serves is either a scene you have not written
or a structural hole you have not noticed. It is the most useful thing in the
report while drafting.

For that to work, the tagging has to be real — the report can only count what
you have linked.

* Understanding Plot Thread Types

Threads are the other half of the file, and they *are* yours to create.

Before capturing plots, understand the different kinds. *These are craft
categories, not property values*: ~:THREAD-TYPE:~ offers only ~Main~ and
~Subplot~, and everything below that is not the main line is a subplot as far
as org-scribe is concerned. What distinguishes one subplot from another is its
~:Stance:~ — the answer to the Theme it embodies — not a letter grade.

** Main Plot (A-Story)

The primary storyline that drives your narrative.

*Characteristics*:
- Present in 60-80% of scenes
- Directly relates to protagonist's main goal
- Reaches climax near story's end
- Determines the genre (thriller, romance, mystery, etc.)

*Examples*:
- Mystery: Solving the murder
- Romance: Will they get together?
- Thriller: Stopping the villain
- Literary: Character's internal transformation

*When to create*: Before drafting - this is your story's spine.

** Major Subplot (B-Story)

A significant secondary storyline.

*Characteristics*:
- Present in 30-50% of scenes
- Often character-focused (relationship, personal growth)
- Intersects with main plot
- Has its own arc and resolution

*Examples*:
- Romance subplot in action story
- Family conflict in mystery novel
- Friendship development
- Secondary character's journey

*When to create*: During planning or early drafting.

** Minor Subplot (C-Story)

A smaller storyline that adds depth.

*Characteristics*:
- Present in 10-30% of scenes
- May or may not resolve fully
- Adds texture and complexity
- Can be thematic or character-focused

*Examples*:
- Protagonist's hobby or side project
- Background political situation
- Minor character's small arc
- Running gag or motif

*When to create*: As they emerge during drafting.

** Thematic Thread

Not a plot in the traditional sense, but a recurring idea or motif.

*Characteristics*:
- Woven throughout story
- Symbolic or philosophical
- No resolution required (but can have payoff)
- Enriches meaning

*Examples*:
- "The meaning of home"
- "Nature vs. technology"
- "Redemption and forgiveness"
- Recurring symbol (bird, color, object)

*When to create*: When you notice the theme emerging.

** Short Story Plot Structure

For short stories (1-10 scenes), you typically have:
- **One main plot** (the story's focus)
- **Optional: One subplot** (adds depth, often character-focused)
- **No minor subplots** (not enough space)

Keep it simple! Short stories thrive on focused narrative.

** Novel Plot Structure

For novels (50-70 scenes), a healthy structure might be:
- **1 main line** (~:THREAD-TYPE: Main~)
- **3-6 subplots** (~:THREAD-TYPE: Subplot~), of varying weight

More than this risks overcomplexity and reader confusion.

A more useful way to decide the number: *one subplot for every Stance you want
represented that the main line does not already carry.* You listed those
Stances in ~design.org~ when you worked out the Theme — at least five, worst
to best — and the characters who embody them are, in the method's phrase, half
your cast. The Stances left over are your subplots. Counted that way, the
number comes out of the book rather than out of a rule of thumb.

* The Plot Thread Capture Template

Let's explore what's in the plot thread capture template.

** Accessing the Template

Press: ~F8 F8 k g~ (F8 twice for the main menu, ~k~ for the capture-entity submenu, then ~g~ for "plot thread")

Or use: ~M-x org-scribe-capture-plot-thread~

** Template Structure

The template is intentionally minimal — a plot-thread capture records the
essence quickly while you are writing; it is not a full planning document:

1. **Properties** — thread type, Stance, status, first-appearance
2. **Stance it embodies / Who carries it** — two lines, at the top of the body
3. **Own desire** — what the thread wants for itself
4. **Crossings with the main line** — three or more, numbered
5. **Resolution** — how it should resolve
6. **Notes** — quick capture notes (can be messy)

Let's examine each in detail.

* Section 1: Properties

When you capture a plot thread using ~org-scribe-capture-plot-thread~, you'll see these properties:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: (generated automatically)
:THREAD-TYPE: Subplot|Main
:Stance:
:STATUS: Planned|In progress|Resolved|Abandoned
:FIRST-APPEARANCE:
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

and this body:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
- Stance it embodies ::
- Who carries it ::

,** Own desire

,** Crossings with the main line

1.
2.
3.

,** Resolution

,** Notes
#+END_EXAMPLE

The same shape is already in ~objects/plot.org~ when the project is created,
under *Main Line* and *Subplot*, so a thread you capture and a thread you fill
in by hand match.

*Plot threads are not the same thing as plot points.* The thirteen
non-negotiable plot points also live in ~objects/plot.org~, at the top, and
they are a fixed list that ships with every project — you tag scenes with them
but never create new ones. Threads are yours to add. Both are covered in this
lesson; the plot points are covered earlier, under "The Thirteen
Non-Negotiables".

** ~:ID:~ (Automatic)

A unique identifier for this plot thread, created automatically.

*Example*: ~plot-main-mystery-20251116-001~

*Purpose*: Enables clickable links from scenes to plot profiles.

*You don't set this* - it's automatic!

** ~:THREAD-TYPE:~

Whether this is the main line of the book or a subplot.

*Values offered at capture*:
- ~Main~ — the main line
- ~Subplot~

*Example*: ~Main~

There are only two, deliberately. A "B-plot" and a "C-plot" are both subplots;
ranking them tells you nothing you can act on, whereas the real question — what
Stance does this thread embody, and where does it cross the main line — is
answered by the two properties below and the Crossings section.

*Note*: this property is what marks the heading as a plot thread for the
linking system. Don't remove it.

** ~:Stance:~

The Stance this thread embodies.

Your Theme is the moral problem of the book; the Stances are the different
answers to it, listed worst to best in ~design.org~. A subplot exists to carry
one of those answers that the main line does not already carry — usually
through the character who embodies it.

*Example*: ~Loyalty is worth more than the truth~

*Tip*: this is the single most useful field on a subplot. If you cannot name
the Stance it embodies, you generally have a sequence of events rather than a
thread — and it will feel detachable to a reader, because it is.

** ~:STATUS:~

Current state of the plot thread.

*Values offered at capture*:
- ~Planned~ — not yet introduced in the manuscript
- ~In progress~ — currently developing
- ~Resolved~ — concluded
- ~Abandoned~ — deliberately dropped

*Example*: ~In progress~

*Tip*: update this as you write, and use ~Abandoned~ honestly rather than
deleting the thread. A dropped thread you can still see is a decision;
a dropped thread you deleted is a loose end you will rediscover in revision.

** ~:FIRST-APPEARANCE:~

When this plot thread first appears in the story.

*Format*: Free text - chapter, scene, or description

*Examples*:
- ~Chapter 1, Scene 1~
- ~Opening scene~
- ~Scene 2 - The Discovery~

*Tip*: Fill this when you write the introduction scene. The template leaves this blank for you to fill in later.

* Section 2: Stance, Carrier and Own Desire

Three short entries at the top of the thread, before any prose. Between them
they decide whether the thread is really a thread.

** ~Stance it embodies~

The answer to the Theme that this thread argues for. Same value as the
~:Stance:~ property — the property is what shows in column view, this line is
where you can write it out properly.

*Examples*:
- "You protect your own, whatever it costs everyone else."
- "The truth is worth more than anybody's comfort."
- "If you cannot fix it, at least do not make it worse."

** ~Who carries it~

The character who embodies that Stance. Usually one person; the thread is the
shape of what happens to them because of what they believe.

If two threads name the same carrier *and* a similar Stance, they are one
thread wearing two hats.

** ~Own desire~

*What this thread wants, on its own terms.*

This is the entry that does the real work, and the template is blunt about the
test it has to pass: **it cannot be "help the protagonist."** A thread whose
desire is to assist the main line is not a subplot, it is a delivery mechanism
— and readers feel it, because nothing in it is ever at risk for its own sake.

Ask what the carrier would still be trying to do if the main plot vanished
overnight. That is the own desire.

*Examples*:
- "Sam wants to be trusted with something that matters, by anyone."
  (/not/ "Sam wants to help Alex investigate")
- "Marcus wants the record corrected before his mother dies."
  (/not/ "Marcus wants to oppose Alex")

** Example: Main Line

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
* The Water Supply Investigation
:PROPERTIES:
:THREAD-TYPE: Main
:Stance: The truth is worth more than anybody's comfort
:STATUS: In progress
:END:

- Stance it embodies :: The truth is worth more than anybody's comfort.
- Who carries it :: Alex Rivera.

,** Desire that drives it

Uncover who is sabotaging the city's water supply before the contamination
spreads citywide — and publish it, whoever it damages.
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Example: Subplot

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
* Sam and the Question of Trust
:PROPERTIES:
:THREAD-TYPE: Subplot
:Stance: Loyalty is worth more than the truth
:STATUS: In progress
:END:

- Stance it embodies :: Loyalty is worth more than the truth.
- Who carries it :: Sam.

,** Own desire

Sam wants to be trusted with something that matters. Not by Alex
specifically — by anyone. Which is exactly why Sam will bury the one fact
that would break the case open.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Notice that the subplot's Stance *contradicts* the main line's. That is the
usual reason a subplot earns its place: it argues the other side, through
someone the reader likes.

* Section 3: Crossings With the Main Line

Where does this thread touch the rest of the book?

** At Least Three, One of Them Late

A subplot's shape is defined by where it *touches* the main line. The template
asks for at least three crossings, numbered, and puts one condition on them:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
At least three crossings with the main line, and one of them at the midpoint,
the apparent defeat or the battle. If it never crosses, it is not a subplot:
it is another novel inside this one.
#+END_QUOTE

Those three named moments are plot points 9, 10 and 12 (see "The Thirteen
Non-Negotiables" above). Requiring a crossing at one of them is what stops a
subplot from running politely alongside the main story and resolving in its own
corner: at the book's highest-pressure moment, the subplot has to be part of
what makes it hard.

** What Counts as a Crossing

Not "both threads appear in this scene." A crossing is a scene where one thread
*changes what happens in the other*:

- The subplot's carrier does something that costs the main line
- The main line forces the subplot's carrier into a choice about their Stance
- A fact from one thread lands in the other and changes its direction

If you can cut the scene and the other thread is unaffected, it was a
co-appearance, not a crossing.

** Example: Subplot Crossings

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Crossings with the main line

1. Scene 18 — Sam vouches for the suspect Alex is about to charge. Alex backs
   off, and the investigation loses three weeks. (Loyalty over truth, at cost.)
2. Scene 24 — Midpoint. Sam is the one who finds the tampering log and does
   not hand it over. The case collapses here because of the subplot, not
   despite it.
3. Scene 42 — Battle. Alex has to decide whether to use what Sam buried,
   knowing it ends the friendship.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Crossing 2 satisfies the condition: it sits at the midpoint, and the main
line's worst moment is caused by the subplot.

** How Much Detail?

- *Minimum*: three numbered crossings, one line each, one of them at the
  midpoint, apparent defeat or battle
- *Standard*: the above, with the scene each one lands in
- *Comprehensive*: the above, plus what each crossing costs the carrier

*Recommendation*: write the three lines when you create the thread. They are
the cheapest structural work in the book, and a subplot that cannot produce
three of them is telling you something now rather than in revision.

* Section 4: Finding a Thread's Scenes

There is no "Key Scenes" section in the template, and that is deliberate — a
hand-maintained list of scenes goes stale the first week of drafting, and a
stale list is worse than none, because you stop checking it against the
manuscript.

Instead, tag each scene with the thread it advances (~F8 F8 g~) and let the
links answer the question whenever you ask it:

| Want                                    | Use                                       |
|-----------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| Every scene on this thread              | ~F8 F8 3~ (search by plot thread)         |
| Which threads run through which chapters | ~F8 F8 L t~ (plot thread report)          |
| Where the gaps are                      | plot timeline and statistics, below       |

All three are current by construction, because they read the manuscript rather
than a list you wrote in chapter 3.

*What to write down instead*: the things a search cannot derive — that a
thread should go quiet for all of Act II, say, or that two of its scenes need
to swap order. Those are notes about *intent*, and they belong under *Notes*.

* Section 5: Resolution

How does this plot thread conclude?

** What to Include

*** Resolution Type
- Definitive (clearly resolved)
- Open-ended (intentionally ambiguous)
- Unresolved (to be continued in sequel)
- Transformed (question changes or becomes irrelevant)

*** Outcome
- What happens?
- How is the central question answered?
- Character state after resolution

*** Timing
- Which scene(s)?
- Placement relative to main climax
- Pacing notes

*** Satisfaction Check
- Does this feel earned?
- Are readers likely to be satisfied?
- Thematic resonance?

** Example: Main Line Resolution

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Resolution

,*** Type
Definitive - the mystery is solved and saboteur caught.

,*** Outcome
Saboteur is revealed to be Marcus Chen, whose family lost everything when
Alex's agency approved the development project 10 years ago. The contamination
was revenge, but also an attempt to expose ongoing corruption.

Alex stops the sabotage and brings Marcus to justice, BUT also reopens
investigation into the original development project, finding evidence of
actual wrongdoing. Justice is served, but Alex realizes the system failed
Marcus first.

,*** Timing
- Revelation: Scene 38 (Chapter 10, end)
- Confrontation: Scene 42 (Chapter 11, climax)
- Legal resolution: Scene 45 (Chapter 12)
- Emotional resolution: Scene 46 (final scene)

,*** Satisfaction
- Mystery solved: YES (who, why, how all answered)
- Stakes addressed: YES (water supply secured, truth exposed)
- Thematic payoff: YES (justice isn't simple, systems need reform)
- Emotional closure: YES (Alex's guilt addressed, growth shown)
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Example: Romance Subplot Resolution

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Resolution

,*** Type
Definitive - relationship status clear, but future open.

,*** Outcome
Alex and Sam confess their feelings (Scene 43) after the climax. They
acknowledge the relationship won't be easy (both have trust issues), but
commit to trying. Story ends with them together, hopeful but realistic.

,*** Timing
- Emotional breakthrough: Scene 40 (Alex vulnerable moment)
- Confession: Scene 43 (post-climax, Act III)
- Final scene together: Scene 46 (hand-holding, looking forward)

,*** Satisfaction
- Will-they-won't-they answered: YES (they will)
- Character arcs complete: YES (Alex learned to trust, Sam learned patience)
- Thematic resonance: YES (connection vs. isolation theme)
- Feels earned: YES (developed across entire novel)
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Section 6: Notes

Writer notes for tracking and continuity.

** What to Include

- Inspiration and references
- Thematic connections
- Challenges or concerns
- Revision reminders
- Scenes that develop this plot (track as you write)
- Research needed
- Alternative endings considered

** Example

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Notes

,*** Inspiration
Loosely inspired by Flint water crisis, but fictionalized. Research:
environmental crime, water treatment systems, investigative journalism.

,*** Thematic Connections
- Connects to "systems vs. individuals" theme
- Parallels Alex's personal arc (trust in institutions broken)
- Water as symbol: life, purity, contamination mirrors character corruption

,*** Challenges
- Risk of being too preachy about environmental issues
- Balance sympathy for saboteur without excusing harm
- Make water treatment science clear but not boring

,*** Scenes Developing This Plot
(Update as I write)
- ✓ Scene 1 - Introduction
- ✓ Scene 4 - First victim
- ✓ Scene 7 - Evidence discovered
- ⚠ Scene 12 - Second site (need more tension here)
- ✓ Scene 18 - False lead
- TODO: Scene 24 - Need midpoint twist
- TODO: Scene 33 - First attack on Alex
- TODO: Scene 38 - Identity reveal
- TODO: Scene 42 - Climax confrontation

,*** Alternative Endings Considered
1. Saboteur dies (too dark, no moral complexity)
2. Saboteur escapes (unsatisfying, no closure)
3. Alex joins saboteur (too extreme for character)
4. CHOSEN: Alex brings to justice but validates grievance

,*** Revision Reminders
- First draft: focus on mystery mechanics
- Second draft: deepen saboteur's motivation (add flashback?)
- Polish: verify water treatment details are accurate
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Capturing a Plot Thread: Step-by-Step

Let's create a plot thread profile together using the implemented capture system.

** Step 1: Invoke Capture

Press: ~F8 F8 k g~ (F8 twice for the main menu, ~k~ for the capture-entity submenu, then ~g~ for "plot thread")

Or use: ~M-x org-scribe-capture-plot-thread~

The capture template opens with prompts.

** Step 2: Fill Template Prompts

The template will prompt you for:

You get three prompts:

1. *Thread Name*: ~Sam and the Question of Trust~
2. *Type*: choose ~Subplot~ or ~Main~
3. *Status*: choose ~Planned~, ~In progress~, ~Resolved~ or ~Abandoned~

An ID is automatically generated using ~%(org-id-new)~ - you don't need to do anything!

~:Stance:~ and ~:FIRST-APPEARANCE:~ are left empty for you to fill in.

** Step 3: Name the Stance and the Carrier

The two lines at the top of the body. Fill them before anything else — if
you cannot, the thread is not ready to exist yet:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
- Stance it embodies :: Loyalty is worth more than the truth.
- Who carries it :: Sam.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Then copy the Stance into the ~:Stance:~ property so it shows in column view.

** Step 4: Own Desire

What this thread wants for itself — remembering that it cannot be "help the
protagonist":

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Own desire

Sam wants to be trusted with something that matters. Not by Alex
specifically — by anyone.
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Step 5: Crossings

At least three, one of them at the midpoint, the apparent defeat or the
battle:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Crossings with the main line

1. Scene 18 — Sam vouches for the suspect; the investigation loses three weeks.
2. Scene 24 (midpoint) — Sam finds the tampering log and does not hand it over.
3. Scene 42 (battle) — Alex must decide whether to use what Sam buried.
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Step 6: Resolution

Plan how the thread resolves:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Resolution

Sam confesses before Alex can expose it. The friendship survives; Sam's
belief that loyalty outranks truth does not.
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Step 7: Notes

Add any quick notes:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,*** Notes

- Research water treatment systems
- Make sure saboteur's motivation is sympathetic
- Parallel Alex's trust issues with water purity theme
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Step 8: Finalize Capture

When done, press: ~C-c C-c~

This saves the plot thread profile to:
- **Novel project**: ~objects/plot.org~, as a *top-level* heading
- **Short story project**: ~notes.org~, nested under the "Plot Threads"
  section (or its Spanish equivalent, matching whichever your project uses)

The level matters in a novel: org-scribe only recognizes a plot thread if its
heading is level 1, the same rule that applies to characters and settings. Do
not demote a thread under another heading — it disappears from completion,
searches and reports.

** Step 9: Verify It Saved

Open your plot file and find your new plot thread entry. In a novel it should
be a level-1 heading with an ID property:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
* Sam and the Question of Trust
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: 20251124-abcd1234
:THREAD-TYPE: Subplot
:Stance: Loyalty is worth more than the truth
:STATUS: In progress
:FIRST-APPEARANCE:
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Setting Up Plot Thread Linking

If you have an existing project with plot threads but no IDs, use the setup wizard.

** Setup Wizard: One-Command Setup

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-setup-plot-thread-links~

This wizard:
1. Adds IDs to all plot threads in your plot file
2. Ensures the capture hook is active for future plot threads
3. Optionally links existing scenes (converts plain text to ID links)

Run this once when setting up ID-based plot thread linking in an existing project.

** Manual Setup Steps

If you prefer to set up manually:

*** Step 1: Add IDs to Plot Threads

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-add-plot-thread-ids~

Opens your plot file (~objects/plot.org~ or ~notes.org~) and adds unique IDs to all plot thread headings that don't have one.

*** Step 2: Link Existing Scenes (Optional)

If you have scenes with plain text plot references:

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-link-all-scene-plot-threads~

Converts all plain text plot references to ID links throughout your manuscript.

Before:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Plot: Main Mystery, Romance
#+END_EXAMPLE

After:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Plot: [[id:abc123][Main Mystery]], [[id:def456][Romance]]
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Linking Plot Threads to Scenes

Once you have plot thread profiles with IDs, link them to scenes.

** Method 1: Set Plot via Menu (Recommended)

1. Open your manuscript (novel.org or story.org)
2. Navigate to a scene heading
3. Press: ~F8 F8 g~ (a top-level Tag Scene action — not a submenu)

Or use: ~M-x org-scribe-set-scene-plot-threads~

4. You'll see a completion menu of all plot threads from your plot file
5. Select plot threads (press ~RET~ on empty line when done)
   - First thread: ~Main Mystery - Water Sabotage~ → ~Enter~
   - Second thread: ~Romance~ → ~Enter~
   - Finish: ~Enter~ (on empty line)
6. The scene's ~:Plot:~ property is updated with ID links

The function reads from:
- Novel: ~objects/plot.org~
- Short story: ~notes.org~ (Plot section)

And automatically creates ID links in the format: ~[[id:abc123][Thread Name]]~

** Method 2: Insert Link at Point

If you're in a property value and want to insert a link:

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-insert-plot-thread-link~

- Single thread selection with completion
- Inserts ID link at cursor: ~[[id:plot-id-here][Plot Name]]~

Or for multiple threads:

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-insert-multiple-plot-thread-links~

- Select multiple threads in sequence
- Press ~RET~ on empty line to finish
- Inserts comma-separated ID links

** Method 3: Converting Existing Plain Text

If you have plain text plot names already in properties:

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-link-scene-plot-threads~

This converts plain text to ID links in the current scene:

Before: ~:Plot: Main Mystery, Romance~
After: ~:Plot: [[id:abc][Main Mystery]], [[id:def][Romance]]~

For all scenes in the buffer:

Use: ~M-x org-scribe-link-all-scene-plot-threads~

** Multiple Plot Threads in One Scene

Many scenes develop multiple plots! Format:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Plot: [[id:plot-main-001][Main Mystery]], [[id:plot-romance-002][Romance]]
#+END_EXAMPLE

Just separate with commas.

** Example

Before:

* Plot Timeline Visualization

Generate a table showing which plot threads appear in which scenes.

** What Is It?

A visual representation of plot thread distribution across your story.

** Creating the Timeline

In your novel.org file, find the "Plot Timeline" section (or create one):

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,* Progress Tracking

,** Plot Timeline

#+BEGIN: plot-thread-timeline
#+END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

Put cursor on ~#+BEGIN:~ line and press: ~C-c C-c~

The dynamic block uses ~org-dblock-write:plot-thread-timeline~ to generate the
table.

*Column order*: threads are sorted by a ~:Weight:~ property, ascending. The
capture asks for it and the template ships it blank; a thread left blank is
treated as weight 999.0, so by default the columns come out alphabetical. If
you want your main line leftmost, give it a number:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Weight: 1.0
#+END_EXAMPLE

Lower sorts first. It is purely a display preference and nothing else reads
it, so use it only if the alphabetical order bothers you.

*Hiding a thread from the table*: a thread you track but don't want a column
for — a running gag, a piece of background business — gets ~-1~ at the Weight
prompt, or by hand:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Weight: -1
#+END_EXAMPLE

Any negative value works; ~-1~ is the documented one. The thread keeps its ID,
its links and its place in every search. Below the table the block writes a
comment line naming what it left out —

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
# Not shown (Weight -1): Running Gag
#+END_EXAMPLE

— so a missing column never has to be a mystery. Being an Org comment, that
line is not exported and not word-counted. To see everything once, add
~:show-hidden t~ to the ~#+BEGIN:~ line and refresh with ~C-c C-c~.

One deliberate limit: *the health report below ignores Weight entirely.* A
hidden thread is still checked for coverage, gaps and single-scene
appearances, and will still produce warnings. Weight decides what the table
shows, not which threads are held to the craft standard — if you want a minor
thread to stop warning you, the question to answer is whether it should be a
thread at all.

** What Good Distribution Looks Like

*** Main Plot (A-Story)
Should appear in 60-80% of scenes, with:
- Consistent presence (no gaps >5 scenes)
- Escalating frequency toward climax
- Present in opening and closing

*** Major Subplot (B-Story)
Should appear in 30-50% of scenes, with:
- Regular but not constant presence
- Can have gaps of 3-5 scenes
- Intersects with main plot periodically
- Resolves before or with main plot

*** Minor Subplot (C-Story)
Should appear in 10-30% of scenes, with:
- Intermittent presence (gaps of 5-10 scenes OK)
- Clusters around specific story sections
- May or may not fully resolve

** Warning Signs

*** Main Plot Issues
- ❌ Missing from 5+ consecutive scenes → Readers forget what's happening
- ❌ Present in <50% of scenes → Not actually the main plot
- ❌ Missing from opening or ending → Structural problem

*** Subplot Issues
- ❌ Appears once, vanishes for 20 scenes, reappears → Feels disconnected
- ❌ Present in >70% of scenes → Might be fighting main plot for dominance
- ❌ Introduced too late (after 40% mark) → Won't have time to develop

*** Overall Structure Issues
- ❌ All plots in every scene → Overcrowded, unfocused
- ❌ Plots never intersect → Story feels like separate stories pasted together
- ❌ Too many plots (>6 total) → Reader confusion

* Common Questions

** Q: Do I need to capture every little storyline?

*A*: No! Only capture plot threads that:
- Span multiple scenes (3+), OR
- Are thematically important, OR
- Need careful tracking to avoid forgetting

You don't need to capture:
- Single-scene conflicts
- Background events with no arc
- Minor obstacles that resolve immediately

** Q: How many plot threads is too many?

*A*:
- **Short story**: 1-2 plots maximum (usually just 1 main plot)
- **Novella**: 2-3 plots (1 main, 1-2 subplots)
- **Novel**: 3-6 plots (1 main, 1-2 major subplots, 1-3 minor subplots)
- **Epic/Series**: Can have more, but not all active simultaneously

If you have more than 6 active plot threads, consider:
- Combining similar plots
- Cutting less important plots
- Resolving some plots midway to make room for new ones

** Q: What if a plot thread changes completely during drafting?

*A*: Update the profile! This is normal. In the Notes section, track the evolution:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Notes

,*** Evolution
- Original plan: Romance between Alex and Sam
- Chapter 5: Realized better chemistry with Jordan
- Changed to: Romance with Jordan, Sam becomes close friend
- Updated: All properties, development arc, resolution plan
#+END_EXAMPLE

The profile should reflect your current story, not your original plan.

** Q: Should subplots resolve at the same time as the main plot?

*A*: Not necessarily! Common patterns:
- **Main plot**: Resolves in climax (Scene 40-45 in 45-scene novel)
- **Character-focused subplot**: Resolves just before or after main plot (Scene 38-43)
- **Romance subplot**: Often resolves just after main plot (Scene 43-44)
- **Minor subplot**: Can resolve earlier (Scene 30-35) or stay open-ended
- **Thematic thread**: Often "resolves" in final reflection scene

What matters: Each plot should feel complete (or intentionally incomplete) by story's end.

** Q: Can I have scenes with no plot threads linked?

*A*: Yes, but sparingly. Common examples:
- Pure character development scenes (though these often advance internal journey plot)
- World-building or atmosphere scenes
- Transitional scenes (travel, setup for next scene)

If >20% of scenes have no plot links, consider:
- Are these scenes necessary?
- Could they advance a subplot while doing their other work?
- Should you create a "character arc" plot thread to link them to?

** Q: What's the difference between a plot thread and a theme?

*A*:
- **Plot thread**: Events that happen, questions that get answered, conflicts that resolve
- **Theme**: Ideas or messages that emerge from the plot

Example:
- Plot: Alex solves murder mystery
- Theme: Justice isn't black and white

You can track themes as "Thematic Threads" in the plot system if you want to ensure they develop throughout the story.

* Troubleshooting

** "I captured a plot thread but can't find it"

*** Problem
The plot thread was captured but doesn't appear in the plot file.

*** Solution
1. Check the correct file:
   - Novel: ~objects/plot.org~
   - Short story: ~notes.org~ (under "Plot Outline")
2. Open the file and search: ~C-s~ then type plot name
3. If not found, the capture might have failed - try again

** "Plot linking menu shows no plot threads"

*** Problem
Using ~org-scribe-set-scene-plot-threads~ shows empty list.

*** Solution
1. Ensure you have captured at least one plot thread
2. Open objects/plot.org or notes.org
3. Verify plot threads have ~:ID:~ properties
4. If IDs are missing, run: ~M-x org-scribe-add-plot-thread-ids~
5. Or run the setup wizard: ~M-x org-scribe-setup-plot-thread-links~

** "Plot health report says 'No scenes found'"

*** Problem
~org-scribe-plot-thread-report~ returns no data or shows "No scenes with Plot properties found."

*** Solution
1. Ensure scenes have ~:Plot:~ properties set
2. Verify plot properties use ID links (not plain text)
3. Check you're running the report from the manuscript file (novel.org or story.org)
4. If plots are plain text, convert them: ~M-x org-scribe-link-all-scene-plot-threads~

** "Plot timeline is blank"

*** Problem
Dynamic block ~#+BEGIN: plot-thread-timeline~ generates but shows no data.

*** Solution
1. Verify at least one scene has a ~:Plot:~ property with ID link
2. Check the plot profiles exist in plot.org and have ~:ID:~ properties
3. Put cursor on ~#+BEGIN: plot-thread-timeline~ and press ~C-c C-c~ again
4. If still blank, check for errors in messages buffer (~*Messages*~)
5. Verify you're using ~plot-thread-timeline~ (not ~plot-timeline~)

** "Search finds no results but I have plot threads"

*** Problem
~org-scribe-org-find-plot~ shows no results even though scenes have Plot properties.

*** Solution
1. Verify Plot properties use ID links: ~[[id:abc][Name]]~
2. If using plain text, the search extracts names from links
3. Try searching for exact plot thread name (case-insensitive)
4. Check that plot threads have matching names in plot.org
5. The search uses substring matching - partial names should work

* Tips for Better Plot Management

** 1. Capture Plot Threads Early

Before you start drafting:
- Identify your main plot (A-story)
- Identify 1-2 major subplots (B-stories)
- Create profiles for these core plots
- Map out key scenes

This provides a roadmap even if you deviate later.

** 2. One Scene = 1-3 Plot Threads Maximum

Each scene should advance:
- Usually 1-2 plot threads
- Occasionally 3 (high-energy scenes)
- Never more than 3 (too much happening)

If a scene tries to advance 4+ plots, split it into two scenes.

** 3. Weave Plots Together

The best scenes advance multiple plots simultaneously:
- Scene develops main mystery AND deepens romance
- Scene resolves minor subplot AND complicates main plot
- Scene develops character arc AND introduces new plot thread

Look for opportunities to make scenes do double duty.

** 4. Use the Rule of Three

For major plot points, use the "rule of three":
- First mention: Plant the seed
- Second mention: Develop it
- Third mention: Payoff

Example - mysterious symbol in mystery plot:
- Scene 5: Alex notices symbol (plant)
- Scene 15: Symbol appears again, Alex investigates (develop)
- Scene 35: Symbol's meaning revealed (payoff)

Track these under *Notes* — or, better, as three crossings, if the motif
belongs to a thread.

** 5. Balance Variety with Focus

Good plot distribution has rhythm:
- Scene 1: Plot A
- Scene 2: Plot A + B
- Scene 3: Plot B + C
- Scene 4: Plot A
- Scene 5: Plot A + B + C
- Scene 6: Plot B

Avoid:
- Too much: A, A, A, A, A (monotonous)
- Too scattered: A+B+C, A+B+C, A+B+C (exhausting)

** 6. Front-Load Introduction

Introduce all major plot threads in first 25% of story:
- Main plot: Scene 1 (or very close)
- Major subplots: Scenes 2-10
- Minor subplots: Scenes 3-15

Introducing new plots after the midpoint is risky - not enough time to develop.

** 7. Escalate Before Resolving

Each plot thread should escalate before resolution:
- Complications increase
- Stakes rise
- Tension builds
- Character struggles more

If a plot just... ends without climax, it feels flat. Build to a peak!

** 8. Resolve in Order of Importance

Common resolution sequence:
1. Minor subplots resolve first (70-80% through)
2. Major subplots resolve next (80-90% through)
3. Main plot resolves in climax (90-95% through)
4. Emotional aftermath and theme in denouement (final 5%)

This creates a cascade effect that feels satisfying.

** 9. Track "Last Mention"

In plot Notes, track when each plot last appeared:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,*** Last Mentions
- Scene 5: Main mystery developed
- Scene 8: Romance subplot (Alex and Sam awkward conversation)
- Scene 12: Main mystery (major discovery!)
- Scene 13: Family conflict mentioned in dialogue
- Scene 17: Main mystery advanced
#+END_EXAMPLE

When writing Scene 20, you can see: "Family conflict hasn't appeared since Scene 13 - maybe reference it?"

** 10. Ask "What's at Stake?"

For each plot thread, regularly check:
- Are the stakes clear?
- Are they high enough?
- Do they escalate?
- Are they personal to the protagonist?

If stakes are unclear or low, the plot feels unimportant even if events happen.

* Summary

Plot threads are the "what happens" of your story - the events, questions, and conflicts that drive your narrative forward. By creating plot thread profiles, you:
- Track where each plot develops across your manuscript
- Ensure balanced development (no forgotten subplots)
- Find gaps where plots disappear too long
- Visualize how plots interweave
- Check that all plots reach satisfying conclusions

The plot linking system uses ID-based links (like characters and locations), allowing you to click from scene properties directly to full plot profiles. Combined with plot timelines and health reports, you have complete visibility into your story's structure.
