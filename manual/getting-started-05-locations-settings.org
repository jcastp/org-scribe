* Welcome to Lesson 5!

In this lesson, you'll learn how to create and manage location profiles for your story's settings. Just like characters, locations can be captured, linked to scenes, and searched throughout your manuscript.

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to:
- Create detailed location profiles using the capture template
- Understand all location properties and when to use them
- Link locations to scenes using ID-based links
- Search for all scenes set in a specific location
- Track setting variety across your story
- Generate location timelines showing when and how locations are used

* Why Capture Locations?

**Quick answer**: Locations ground your story in physical space and help maintain consistency.

** The Problem Without Location Tracking

When writing without location tracking, you might:
- Describe a coffee shop as "small and cozy" in Chapter 1, then "spacious with high ceilings" in Chapter 5
- Forget which scenes happen in the protagonist's apartment
- Realize you've set 15 consecutive scenes in the same location (boring!)
- Struggle to remember what details you've revealed about a location
- Have inconsistent geography (the park that's sometimes downtown, sometimes uptown)

** The Solution: Location Profiles

Location profiles help you:
1. **Maintain Consistency**: Reference your description instead of contradicting yourself
2. **Track Usage**: See how often you use each setting
3. **Balance Variety**: Ensure you're not overusing certain locations
4. **Quick Reference**: Remember key details when writing a scene
5. **World-Building**: Build a coherent fictional geography
6. **Search**: Find all scenes in a specific location instantly

* Understanding Location Types

Before capturing locations, understand the different types:

** Primary Locations

Settings that appear frequently throughout your story.

*Examples*:
- Protagonist's home or apartment
- Main workplace
- School or university
- Central gathering place (coffee shop, park, etc.)

*When to create*: Before or during early drafting.

** Secondary Locations

Settings that appear multiple times but less frequently.

*Examples*:
- Friend's apartment
- Favorite restaurant
- Subway station
- Library

*When to create*: As you write scenes in these locations.

** Minor Locations

Settings that appear once or twice but are important to remember.

*Examples*:
- Hotel lobby for a key conversation
- Rooftop where a crucial event happens
- Specific street corner

*When to create*: When consistency matters even for brief appearances.

** Atmospheric Locations

Settings that define your story's mood or world.

*Examples*:
- The haunted mansion (horror)
- The starship bridge (sci-fi)
- The medieval castle (fantasy)
- The corporate office (contemporary drama)

*When to create*: Early in planning - these shape your story's feel.

* The Location Capture Template

Let's explore what's in the location capture template.

** Accessing the Template

Press: ~F8 F8 k l~ (F8 twice for the main menu, ~k~ for the capture-entity submenu, then ~l~ for "location")

Or use: ~M-x org-scribe-capture-location~

** Template Structure

Here is what the capture template really inserts, verbatim:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,* Setting name
3:PROPERTIES:
:ID: (generated automatically)
:Type:
:Echo:
:First-appearance:
:END:

- Echo ::
- Hazard ::
- Character ::
- Symbol ::

,** Description

,** Scenes that happen here

,** Notes
#+END_EXAMPLE

It is short on purpose. Three properties and four one-line Aspects carry
almost everything; the prose section is for what you can see, hear and smell
when you are standing there.

The settings file that ships with a new novel project
(~objects/locations.org~) uses the same shape, so a setting you capture and a
setting you fill in by hand look identical.

*The important idea*: a setting is not a backdrop. It has a job in the
argument of the story, and the four Aspects are that job written down. A
setting with no Aspect at all is scenery — and in the revision passes,
settings used only as scenery are candidates for cutting.

* Section 1: Properties

When you capture a setting, you'll see these three properties:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: (generated automatically)
:Type:
:Echo:
:First-appearance:
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

** ~:ID:~ (Automatic)

A unique identifier for this setting, created automatically.

*Example*: ~loc-coffee-shop-20251116-001~

*Purpose*: Enables clickable links from scenes to setting profiles.

*You don't set this* - it's automatic!

** ~:Type:~

What kind of place this is. The capture prompt is free text, so use whatever
categories suit your story — the property exists so that org-scribe can tell a
setting heading from anything else in the file, and so you can group settings
in column view.

*Examples*: ~Residence~, ~Business~, ~Public~, ~Institution~, ~Natural~, ~Transport~

*Tip*: Use broad categories. The specifics belong in the description.

*Note*: this property is what makes the heading a setting as far as the
linking system is concerned. Don't delete it, and don't put the category in
the heading — the heading is the *name* of the place.

** ~:Echo:~

A short restatement of the setting's Echo (see below), kept in a property so
it shows up in column view beside the setting's name. The shipped
~#+COLUMNS:~ line in ~objects/locations.org~ displays exactly three things:
the name, the ~:Type:~, and this.

*Tip*: keep it to a few words here and write the full version in the ~Echo ::~
line in the body.

** ~:First-appearance:~

When readers first see this setting.

*Format*: ~Chapter X, Scene Y~ or ~Scene title~

*Examples*:
- ~Chapter 1, Scene 2~
- ~Scene 3 - The Coffee Shop Encounter~
- ~Opening scene~

*Tip*: Fill this when you link the setting to its first scene.

** Properties that no longer exist

Earlier versions of the templates had ~:Importance:~, ~:Climate:~,
~:Population:~, ~:Setting:~, ~:Accessibility:~, ~:Owner:~ and ~:Atmosphere:~.
They are gone. Nothing reads them, and adding them back does nothing.

Most of what they held has a better home:

| Old property           | Where it goes now                                           |
|------------------------+-------------------------------------------------------------|
| ~:Atmosphere:~           | the *Echo* and *Symbol* Aspects — mood with a job to do         |
| ~:Importance:~           | how often you actually link the setting to scenes           |
| ~:Owner:~                | *Description*, or a character link in the scene that shows it |
| ~:Accessibility:~      | *Description*, when it constrains a scene                     |
| ~:Setting:~              | ~objects/worldbuilding.org~, which holds the world as a whole |
| ~:Climate:~ ~:Population:~ | ~objects/worldbuilding.org~                                   |

* Section 2: The Four Aspects

This is the heart of your setting profile.

The four Aspects sit at the top of the profile, before any prose. Each is one
line. They are what turn a place into part of the story's argument rather than
a stage it happens on, and they are the reason this template is so much shorter
than a generic "location profile" — four sharp lines do more work than three
paragraphs of description.

Write them in the order given: the Echo comes from your Theme, and the rest
lean on it.

** Echo

*How this place reflects the Theme.*

Your Theme is the moral problem of the book, written down in ~design.org~. The
Echo is that problem showing up in the physical world — in what the place
allows, rewards or punishes.

This is the Aspect that makes a setting part of the argument instead of a
backdrop. If you can't write it, ask whether you need the place at all, or
whether a scene set here could happen anywhere else with no loss.

*Examples*:
- ~Everyone here speaks between the lines.~
- ~Nobody is ever alone long enough to think.~
- ~The rules are posted, and nobody follows them.~

** Hazard

*How this place makes the World Problem worse.*

The World Problem is the pressure the world puts on everyone in it, also
recorded in ~design.org~. The Hazard is this place's contribution to it —
what it makes harder, riskier or more costly.

The Hazard is what generates scenes. A place with a real Hazard produces
conflict on its own, without you having to arrange for someone to walk in with
a gun.

*Examples*:
- ~Everyone is looking for someone to pin the blame on.~
- ~Anything said here reaches the wrong ears by evening.~
- ~You cannot leave without being seen leaving.~

** Character

*A character Aspect this place activates.*

Characters carry Aspects too (Lesson 4). This line names the one that this
place brings out — the trait, debt or history that becomes unavoidable here.

It is what makes a setting matter to a *specific* person. The same room can be
neutral for one character and intolerable for another, and that difference is
usually where the scene is.

*Examples*:
- ~I owe money to half the people in this room.~
- ~This is where I was told I would never amount to anything.~
- ~Everyone here still calls me by my father's name.~

** Symbol

*The object or detail that charges the scene.*

One concrete thing the reader can see. Not a mood — a noun. It gives you
something to cut back to, and it carries the Echo without anyone having to
state it.

*Examples*:
- ~The cup I never get to drink.~
- ~The clock that has been ten minutes fast for years.~
- ~The chair nobody sits in.~

** Example: Coffee Shop

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,* Downtown Coffee Collective
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: loc-coffee-shop-20251116-001
:Type: Business
:Echo: Belonging you have to keep paying for
:First-appearance: Chapter 1, Scene 1
:END:

- Echo :: Everyone here belongs, as long as they keep buying something.
- Hazard :: Regulars notice everything, and none of them keep it to themselves.
- Character :: Alex's need to be recognized without having to ask.
- Symbol :: The chalkboard with the regulars' names on it.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Note how much narrower this is than "a warm bohemian coffee shop". The Echo
commits the place to the book's argument, the Hazard guarantees the place
generates trouble, and the Symbol gives every scene set here something to
point at.

*A setting with no Aspect at all is scenery.* That is not a rule invented for
the template — it is a revision test. When you reach the revision passes,
settings with no Aspects are the first candidates for cutting, because nothing
would be lost by moving their scenes somewhere else.

* Section 3: Description

What can be seen, heard and smelled — and *only what a focalizer would notice
while standing there*.

That constraint is the whole point of the section. You are not writing a
surveyor's report; you are writing what a particular person perceives, which
means the details are already filtered by who they are and what they want.
Description written this way can be lifted almost directly into a scene.

** What to Include

- What the point-of-view character would actually register on walking in
- Sound and smell, not only sight — they are the senses prose usually forgets
- Details that the Aspects above have made relevant

** Example: Coffee Shop

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Description

Narrow and deep, brick walls, reclaimed wood floors. Mismatched vintage chairs
around small round tables. The back wall is floor-to-ceiling bookshelves —
customers leave books, take books. Large front windows: flooded with light in
the morning, dim by mid-afternoon.

The espresso machine dominates the counter, chrome and copper, always hissing.
Fresh beans, cinnamon from the pastry case, wet coats when it rains. Indie
music low, ceramic clinking, the door chime.

Alex notices the chalkboard before anything else, every single time.
#+END_EXAMPLE

** How Much Detail?

Less than you think. The Aspects carry the meaning; this section only has to
give you enough concrete material to write from.

- *Minimum*: dominant sensory impression, plus whatever the Symbol needs
- *Standard*: the above, plus layout and two or three senses
- *Comprehensive*: the above, plus how it changes with time of day or season

*Recommendation*: write the minimum at capture time, and expand the first time
you actually set a scene there. Description written before you need it tends
to be description you never use.

* Section 4: Scenes That Happen Here

A list of the scenes set in this place.

You do not maintain this by hand. Tag each scene with its ~Location~ property
(~F8 F8 l~) and the link runs from the scene to the setting; searching by
location (~F8 F8 4~, covered later in this lesson) gives you the list on
demand, current by construction.

Keep the section for anything the search cannot tell you — a note that three
scenes here all happen in the same week, say, or that the place is used twice
in Act I and then never again.

* Section 5: Notes

Writer notes for continuity and reminders.

** What to Include

- Continuity details (what has been revealed to the reader, and when)
- Future plans (things that must happen here later)
- Research needed
- Real-world inspiration
- Changes over time, if the place evolves

** Example: Coffee Shop

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Notes

,*** Revealed to Reader
- [Chapter 1] Front windows, espresso machine, brick walls, chalkboard
- [Chapter 3] Bookshelf wall
- [Chapter 5] Alex's usual table (back left corner)

,*** To Reveal Later
- Back alley entrance (needed for the Chapter 12 escape)

,*** Evolution
Chapter 14: they renovate. New paint, same furniture, and the chalkboard
comes down — which is the Symbol going out with it, deliberately.
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Capturing a Location: Step-by-Step

Let's create a location profile together.

** Step 1: Invoke Capture

Press: ~F8 F8 k l~

You'll see: ~Capture location name:~

** Step 2: Enter Location Name

Type: ~Downtown Coffee Collective~

Press: ~Enter~

The capture template opens in a new buffer.

** Step 3: Fill Properties

You get two prompts, and that is all:

1. ~Type~ → type ~Business~
2. ~First appearance~ → press ~Enter~ to leave it blank for now (fill it in
   when you link the setting to its first scene)

Press ~Enter~ to accept each one. The ~:ID:~ is generated for you, and
~:Echo:~ is left empty for you to fill in after you have written the Echo
Aspect in the body.

** Step 4: Fill In the Aspects

Point lands in the *Description* section, but do the four Aspects first —
they are what the rest hangs on:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
- Echo :: Everyone here belongs, as long as they keep buying something.
- Hazard :: Regulars notice everything, and none of them keep it to themselves.
- Character :: Alex's need to be recognized without having to ask.
- Symbol :: The chalkboard with the regulars' names on it.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Then copy a shortened version of the Echo up into the ~:Echo:~ property, so it
shows in column view.

*If you can only manage one*, make it the Echo — it is the one that decides
whether the place belongs in the book. The others can wait until you set a
scene there.

Finally, write a few lines under *Description*: what a point-of-view character
would notice standing in the room. ~Notes~ can stay empty.

** Step 5: Finalize Capture

When done, press: ~C-c C-c~

This saves the location profile to:
- **Novel project**: ~objects/locations.org~
- **Short story project**: ~notes.org~ (under "Setting → Locations")

** Step 6: Verify It Saved

Open your locations file and find your new location entry. It should be a level-1 heading with an ID.

* Linking Locations to Scenes

Once you have location profiles, link them to scenes.

** Method 1: Set Location via Menu (Recommended)

1. Open your manuscript (novel.org or story.org)
2. Navigate to a scene heading
3. Press: ~F8 F8 l~ (a top-level Tag Scene action — not a submenu)

Or use: ~M-x org-scribe-set-scene-locations~

4. You'll see a menu of all locations
5. Select one: Press ~Enter~
6. The scene's ~:Location:~ property is updated with an ID link

** Method 2: Manual Linking

If you prefer typing:

1. Navigate to scene properties
2. Find or add: ~:Location:~
3. Type: ~[[id:location-id-here][Location Name]]~

This requires knowing the location's ID. The menu method is easier!

** Example

Before:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,*** Scene 1 - Morning Routine
:PROPERTIES:
:PoV: [[id:char-alex-001][Alex]]
:Location: coffee shop
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

After (with menu):
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,*** Scene 1 - Morning Routine
:PROPERTIES:
:PoV: [[id:char-alex-001][Alex]]
:Location: [[id:loc-coffee-001][Downtown Coffee Collective]]
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Navigating Between Scenes and Locations

** Jump to Location Profile

When in a scene with a linked location:

1. Move cursor to the ~:Location:~ property line
2. Press: ~C-c C-o~ (org-open-at-point)

You jump to the full location profile! (Unlike PoV characters and plot threads, there is no dedicated hydra key for jumping to a location — org's native link-following is the way.)

** Return to Scene

After viewing the location profile:

Press: ~C-c &~ (org-mark-ring-goto)

You return to your original scene.

** Why This Is Useful

While writing:
- "Wait, what color are the walls?" → Jump to profile, check, return
- "How does this place smell again?" → Quick reference
- "Who owns this place?" → Check property

* Searching for Scenes by Location

Find all scenes set in a specific location.

** Command

Press: ~F8 F8 4~ (search by location)

Or use: ~M-x org-scribe-org-find-location~

** Process

1. You'll see: ~Search for location:~
2. Start typing location name: ~Coffee~
3. Matching locations appear
4. Select one: ~Downtown Coffee Collective~
5. Press: ~Enter~

** Results

A buffer opens showing ALL scenes with that location:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Results for location "Downtown Coffee Collective":

,*** Scene 1 - Morning Routine
    Chapter 1: The Beginning

,*** Scene 5 - Unexpected Meeting
    Chapter 2: Complications

,*** Scene 12 - Confrontation
    Chapter 4: Crisis Point

Found 3 scenes.
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Use Cases

- "Where have I used this location?" → See full list
- "Am I overusing this setting?" → Count scenes
- "What's happened here so far?" → Review all scenes
- "Continuity check" → Ensure consistent descriptions

* Tracking Setting Variety

Avoid setting too many scenes in the same location.

** The Problem

It's easy to default to the same settings:
- 10 scenes in Alex's apartment
- 8 scenes in the coffee shop
- 2 scenes everywhere else combined

This gets boring for readers (and you!).

** The Solution: Location Audit

Periodically check location distribution:

1. Search each primary location (F8 F8 4)
2. Count scenes per location
3. Identify overused settings
4. Plan scenes in underused locations

** Healthy Distribution (Novel)

For a novel with 50-70 scenes:
- 3-5 primary locations: 5-10 scenes each
- 5-10 secondary locations: 2-4 scenes each
- 10-20 minor locations: 1 scene each

** Example Audit

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Location Audit (30 scenes written):

Primary:
- Coffee shop: 6 scenes ✓ (balanced)
- Alex's apartment: 11 scenes ⚠ (too many!)
- Office: 4 scenes ✓ (balanced)

Secondary:
- Sam's bookstore: 3 scenes ✓
- Washington Park: 2 scenes ✓
- Subway: 1 scene ✓

Minor:
- Jordan's loft: 1 scene ✓
- Art gallery: 1 scene ✓
- Rooftop bar: 1 scene ✓

Action: Write next 3 scenes outside Alex's apartment!
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Converting Existing Scenes

Already have scenes with plain-text locations?

** Convert Individual Scene

1. Navigate to scene with plain-text location
2. Run: ~M-x org-scribe-link-scene-locations~ (not directly hydra-bound)

This finds the location name in your profiles and replaces it with an ID link.

** Convert All Scenes (Batch)

The easiest path is ~F8 F8 R~ (or ~F8 F8 L R~) — ~org-scribe-relink-project~ converts plain-text names to ID links (and adds missing IDs, and refreshes display names) across *every* entity type, including locations, in one pass.

If you specifically want only locations converted, run ~M-x org-scribe-link-all-scene-locations~ directly.

This processes your entire manuscript, converting all matching location names to ID links.

*Warning*: Review results! Ambiguous names might match incorrectly.

* Practical Exercise (15 minutes)

Let's practice location management end-to-end.

** Part 1: Capture Two Locations (7 minutes)

*** Location 1: Home Setting

1. Press: ~F8 F8 k l~
2. Name: ~[Protagonist's Apartment/House]~
3. Fill properties:
   - Type: Residence
   - First appearance: leave blank
4. Write the four Aspects — one line each:
   - Echo: how this place reflects your Theme
   - Hazard: how it makes things worse for whoever is in it
   - Character: what it brings out in the protagonist
   - Symbol: one concrete object you could cut back to
5. Write 1 paragraph of description: what the protagonist notices here
6. Press: ~C-c C-c~ to save

*** Location 2: Public Setting

1. Press: ~F8 F8 k l~
2. Name: ~[Park/Restaurant/Library/Store - your choice]~
3. Fill properties:
   - Type: Public or Business
   - First appearance: leave blank
4. Write the four Aspects again — and make this place's *Hazard* pull in the
   opposite direction from Location 1's. Two settings that endanger a
   character in the same way are one setting
5. Write 1 paragraph of description
6. Press: ~C-c C-c~ to save

** Part 2: Link to Scenes (4 minutes)

1. Open your manuscript
2. Find or create a scene
3. Press: ~F8 F8 l~ (set scene locations)
4. Select Location 1
5. Verify the ~:Location:~ property now has an ID link
6. Navigate to another scene
7. Repeat with Location 2

** Part 3: Test Navigation and Search (4 minutes)

*** Test Navigation:
1. In a scene with linked location
2. Press: ~C-c C-o~ on the Location property
3. Jump to location profile → Success!
4. Press: ~C-c &~ to return → Success!

*** Test Search:
1. Press: ~F8 F8 4~
2. Search for one of your locations
3. See results → Should show scene(s) you just linked
4. Press ~q~ to close results

** Success Criteria

✓ You captured 2 location profiles with IDs
✓ Location files (objects/locations.org or notes.org) contain your locations
✓ At least 2 scenes have linked locations in their properties
✓ You can jump from scene to location profile and back
✓ Search finds scenes by location

* Integration with Your Daily Writing Workflow

** During Planning

- Capture primary locations before drafting
- Include sensory details for immersion
- Note significance and connections
- Reference when outlining scenes

** During Drafting

- Before writing a scene in a location:
  1. Jump to location profile
  2. Review sensory details
  3. Check continuity notes
  4. Return and write scene
- After using a location for first time:
  - Fill in ~:First-appearance:~ property
- When introducing a new location mid-draft:
  - Pause, press F8 F8 k l
  - Quick capture (5-minute version)
  - Link to scene immediately

** During Revision

- Search each location to check consistency:
  - Are descriptions consistent?
  - Do sensory details match?
  - Any contradictions?
- Check variety:
  - Audit location distribution
  - Rewrite/move scenes if overusing a location
- Update profiles:
  - Add details revealed during drafting
  - Update Notes section with what reader knows

** Weekly Maintenance

- Review location timeline
- Note locations that need more development
- Check for forgotten settings
- Plan scenes in underused locations

* Common Questions

** Q: Do I need a profile for every location?

*A*: No! Only locations that:
- Appear more than once, OR
- Have important story significance, OR
- Need consistent description

You don't need profiles for:
- Brief mentions ("they walked past a hardware store")
- Generic settings ("a restaurant" with no specific details)
- One-line locations ("the sidewalk")

** Q: How detailed should the Description be?

*A*: Less detailed than you expect. The four Aspects carry the meaning, so the
description only has to give you concrete material to write from — enough to
visualize the space, reach for a sound or a smell mid-scene, and stay
consistent about it.

For most settings a short paragraph is plenty. Go deeper only for places the
reader spends real time in, and preferably *after* you have written a scene
there, when you know which details you actually reached for.

** Q: What if my location changes during the story?

*A*: Update the profile! In the Notes section, track the evolution:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Notes

,*** Evolution
- [Chapter 1-5]: Messy, dishes in sink, laundry everywhere
- [Chapter 6]: Alex cleans up (turning point)
- [Chapter 7-12]: Tidy, plants on windowsill, organized
#+END_EXAMPLE

This helps you remember which version of the location exists at which point in the story.

** Q: Can I have multiple scenes in "Apartment" without being more specific?

*A*: Yes, but consider breaking it down:
- Alex's Apartment - Living Room
- Alex's Apartment - Bedroom
- Alex's Apartment - Kitchen

Each can have its own profile with specific details. This provides more variety and clearer staging for scenes.

** Q: Should I link generic locations like "Street" or "Car"?

*A*: Usually no. Link specific instances:
- "Main Street" (if it's a recurring setting)
- "Alex's Car" (if it's important and reappears)
- "The Red Line Train" (if it's a regular location)

Don't link generic "a street" or "a car" - those don't need consistency tracking.

** Q: How do I handle locations that only exist in one scene but are important?

*A*: Create a profile with:
- A sharp Echo and Symbol — a place that carries one crucial scene needs those
  more than a recurring one does
- Detailed description (since it's important)
- A note under *Notes* on why this single scene matters

Example: The rooftop where the protagonist makes a crucial decision - appears once, but needs vivid description and emotional weight.

* Troubleshooting

** "I captured a location but can't find it"

*** Problem
The location was captured but doesn't appear in the locations file.

*** Solution
1. Check the correct file:
   - Novel: ~objects/locations.org~
   - Short story: ~notes.org~ (under "Setting → Locations")
2. Open the file and search: ~C-s~ then type location name
3. If not found, the capture might have failed - try again

** "Location linking menu shows no locations"

*** Problem
Pressing F8 F8 l shows empty list.

*** Solution
1. Ensure you have captured at least one location
2. Open objects/locations.org or notes.org
3. Verify locations are level-1 headings with ~:ID:~ properties
4. If IDs are missing, run: ~M-x org-scribe-add-location-ids~

** "Search finds no scenes for a location I know I linked"

*** Problem
Search returns 0 results despite linked scenes.

*** Solution
1. Open a scene that should have this location
2. Check the ~:Location:~ property
3. Verify it's an ID link: ~[[id:...][Name]]~
4. If it's plain text, run: ~M-x org-scribe-link-scene-locations~
5. Try search again

** "Jump to location doesn't work"

*** Problem
~C-c C-o~ on Location property doesn't jump.

*** Solution
1. Ensure the location property is an ID link (has ~[[id:...]]~ syntax)
2. Put cursor directly on the link text
3. Verify the location profile still exists and has that ID
4. If the ID is orphaned, re-link manually

* Tips for Better Location Management

** 1. Capture Early, Fill Later

When a new location appears in your draft:
- Immediately capture it (just name + basic properties)
- Fill detailed description later during revision
- This prevents forgetting about the location

** 2. Use Real-World Inspiration

Base fictional locations on real places:
- Visit and take notes (or use memory)
- Photograph details
- Record sensory impressions
- Change enough to make it yours

Reference the real place in your Notes section.

** 3. Create a Mental Map

For complex settings (entire neighborhoods, buildings with multiple rooms):
- Note spatial relationships under *Notes* ("upstairs from", "across the
  street from")
- Consider sketching a map (store in Notes as an image link)
- If the whole geography matters to the book, it belongs in
  ~objects/worldbuilding.org~ rather than in any one setting's profile

** 4. Balance Familiarity and Novelty

- Familiar locations (home, workplace) → Less description, more emotion
- Novel locations (first-time visits) → More description, character's reaction

Update your profile to note which scenes establish vs. revisit the location.

** 5. Leverage Sensory Details

For each location, identify its signature sense:
- Coffee shop → Smell (coffee, pastries)
- Office → Sound (phones, keyboards, air conditioning)
- Forest → Touch (rough bark, cool air)
- Beach → Sound (waves, gulls) + Touch (sand, wind)

Lead with that sense when writing scenes there.

** 6. Track Revealed Details

In the Notes section, maintain a list of what readers know:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,*** Revealed to Reader
- [Chapter 1] Front door is blue
- [Chapter 3] Kitchen has large window
- [Chapter 5] Creaky floorboard in hallway
- [Chapter 8] Bookshelf in living room
#+END_EXAMPLE

This prevents:
- Revealing the same detail twice ("As if for the first time")
- Contradicting earlier descriptions

** 7. Use Location as Character

Important locations can function like characters:
- They have personality (atmosphere)
- They affect characters emotionally
- They can change over time
- They participate in the story

Treat your protagonist's home or a central gathering place with the same care as a secondary character.

** 8. Audit Regularly

Every 10-15 scenes, run a location audit:
- Which locations are dominating?
- Which haven't been used in 20+ scenes?
- Are there too many one-off locations?
- Do you need to consolidate similar locations?

** 9. Connect Locations Thematically

Group locations by theme or function:
- "Safe spaces" vs. "danger zones"
- "Public" vs. "private"
- "Past" (childhood locations) vs. "present"
- "Work life" vs. "home life"

This grouping is really a question about Echoes: two settings in the same group
usually echo the Theme from the same side. Compare their ~Echo ::~ lines, and
if they say the same thing, consider whether you need both.

** 10. Let Locations Surprise You

Sometimes you'll invent a location for a single scene and it becomes important:
- A background location becomes a key setting
- A minor location reveals character depth
- A throwaway scene in a new place sparks plot ideas

When this happens, upgrade the setting:
- Fill in the Aspects you skipped — a place carrying real weight needs all four
- Expand the description
- Plan additional scenes there

* Summary

Locations ground your story in physical space and help readers visualize your world. By creating location profiles, you:
- Maintain consistency across multiple scenes
- Track setting variety to avoid repetition
- Quick-reference details while drafting
- Build a coherent fictional geography
- Search instantly for all scenes in a location

The location linking system uses ID-based links, just like characters, allowing you to click from scene properties directly to full location profiles. This makes it easy to check details, ensure consistency, and manage complex settings.

With location management in place, your story now has both *who* (characters) and *where* (locations) fully tracked. Next, you'll learn to track *what happens* with plot threads!
