* Welcome to Lesson 4!

Your story is full of people (or aliens, or dragons, or talking furniture...). Keeping track of them all can be challenging! This lesson teaches you how to create detailed character profiles and link them to your scenes.

By the end of this lesson, you'll have:
- Created your first character profile using the capture template
- Understood all sections of the character template
- Learned how the ID-based linking system works
- Set PoV and character properties with one click
- Searched for all scenes featuring a character
- Generated a character timeline showing who appears where

*Estimated time*: 20 minutes

* Before You Begin

** What You Need

✓ A project with at least one scene written
✓ At least one character in your story
✓ Your manuscript file and notes/plan files accessible

** Quick Check

Think about your main character:
- What's their name?
- What role do they play? (protagonist, antagonist, supporting)
- What do they want?

You don't need all the details yet - we'll develop them together!

* Why Character Profiles Matter

Before we start capturing characters, let's understand why this helps your writing.

** The Problem Without Profiles

When characters live only in your head or scattered through scenes:
- "What color were Alex's eyes again?"
- "Wait, is Sam 32 or 35?"
- "Did I ever mention Jordan's backstory?"
- "Which scenes actually feature Maria?"
- "I haven't shown my antagonist in 10 chapters!"

** The Solution: Centralized Profiles

With character profiles:
- ✓ All details in one place (no inconsistencies!)
- ✓ Click from scenes to full character info
- ✓ Search for all scenes featuring a character
- ✓ Track character appearances across your story
- ✓ Plan character arcs systematically
- ✓ Never forget supporting characters

** Real-World Benefits

*During drafting*:
- Quick reference: "What's Alex's occupation again?"
- Consistency: Same eye color every time
- Development: Arc planning keeps character growth on track

*During revision*:
- Find all Alex scenes (check PoV balance)
- Find all scenes with Alex AND Sam (check relationship development)
- Generate character timeline (ensure protagonists appear regularly)
- Identify "forgotten" characters (only appear once?)

* Understanding Character Files

Before capturing, let's see where characters live.

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE

* Capturing Your First Character

Let's create a character profile using the capture template!

** Step 1: Activate Character Capture

From anywhere in your project:

1. Press ~F8 F8~ (Hydra menu)
2. Press ~k~ (Capture entity submenu)
3. Press ~h~ (for "cHaracter" - yes, the 'h' is a bit weird, but 'c' was taken!)

The menu closes and you see a prompt.

** Step 2: Enter Character Name

You'll see:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Character Name:
#+END_EXAMPLE

Type your character's name (example: ~Alex Rivera~) and press Enter.

** Step 3: Fill in the Prompts

The template asks for key information. Fill each prompt:

1. *Role*: choose from ~Protagonist~, ~Opponent~, ~Ally~, ~Fake-Ally Opponent~,

   - Example: ~28~

3. *Occupation*: What they do for a living

4. *First appearance*: When they first appear

Press Enter after each prompt.

* Understanding the Character Template

Let's explore each section and what to put in it.

** The Properties Block

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: char-alex-rivera-20251116-001
:Role: Protagonist
:Stance:
:Goal:
:Age: 28
:Occupation: Freelance graphic designer
:First-appearance: Chapter 1
:RelationshipsData:
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

*** ~:ID:~ - The Magic Link (Auto-Generated!)

This unique identifier is *automatically created* and is the key to the linking system.

*What it does*:
- Creates a unique ID for this character
- Enables linking from scenes to this profile
- Allows searching and navigation
- Never changes even if you rename the character

*Important*: Don't delete or modify this! The system generated it for you.

*** ~:Role:~ - Character Function

Your character's function in the story:

- *Protagonist* — the story is about them
- *Opponent* — wants the same thing, and is the reason the protagonist cannot
  simply have it
- *Ally* — with the protagonist, and usually the one who says the thing they
  do not want to hear
- *Fake-Ally Opponent* — appears to be an ally and is not
- *Thematic Supporting* — exists to embody one of the Stances on the Theme

The last two are worth pausing on. They are not "minor characters": each has a
job in the argument of the book. A Thematic Supporting character is how a
Stance you are not going to give a subplot still gets stated out loud by
somebody who believes it.

*Note*: this property is what marks the heading as a character for the linking
system. Keep it, and keep the *name* in the heading — the role does not belong
there. That separation is why renaming a character never breaks a link.

*** ~:Stance:~ - Their Answer to the Theme

Where this character stands on the moral problem of the book, in their own
words. Your Theme and its Stances are in ~design.org~ (Lesson 1).

This is the most useful property on the sheet. It is what tells you whether
you have a cast or a crowd: two characters with the same Stance are, for the
book's purposes, the same character, however different their jobs and haircuts.

Pick, for each character, the Stance that generates the most conflict with the
protagonist's.

*** ~:Goal:~ - What They Want, in the Property

A short form of their Desire's Goal (below), kept here so it shows in column
view.

Try ~C-c C-x C-c~ in ~objects/characters.org~: the shipped ~#+COLUMNS:~ line
displays Character, Role, Stance and Goal side by side. That view — everyone's
position and everyone's want, on one screen — is the fastest way to see whether
your cast is actually pulling in different directions.

*** Other Properties

The template filled these from your prompts:
- ~:Age:~ - Character's age
- ~:Occupation:~ - What they do
- ~:First-appearance:~ - When they show up

~:RelationshipsData:~ is managed for you by the relationship commands
(covered later in this lesson) — you don't edit it by hand.

You can edit the rest anytime.

*** Optional: ~:Weight:~ for Timeline Order

The capture asks you for this one, and the template ships it blank. It controls
column order in the character timeline: ~1.0~ to ~5.0~, lower first. Press
~RET~ at the prompt to leave it blank — a blank weight is treated as 999.0, so
the character sorts last, alphabetically among the other blanks. That is the
default, and it is why an untouched timeline comes out alphabetical.

It is a display preference. Nothing else reads it — not the searches, not the
health report.

*** Optional: ~:Weight: -1~ to Keep a Character Out of the Timeline

A novel accumulates walk-on parts — the innkeeper with two lines, the desk
sergeant, the neighbour's dog. You may well want a file entry for each so you
can keep their names straight, and yet not want a column for each of them in
the timeline, where thirty near-empty columns bury the ten that matter.

Answer ~-1~ at the Weight prompt when you capture them, or add it by hand later:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Weight: -1
#+END_EXAMPLE

They keep their profile, their ID, their links from scenes, and their place in
every search and report. They simply stop getting a column. Any negative value
works, but ~-1~ is the one to use.

The timeline does not hide the fact that it is hiding something. Below the
table it writes a line naming who was left out:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
# Not shown (Weight -1): Desk Sergeant, Innkeeper
#+END_EXAMPLE

That line starts with ~#~, which makes it an Org comment: it is visible to you
in the buffer, and invisible to exports and to word counts — the same trick the
~*EDIT*~ markers use.

To see the full table once without editing any properties, add ~:show-hidden t~
to the block header and refresh with ~C-c C-c~:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,#+BEGIN: character-timeline :show-hidden t
,#+END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

The same convention works for plot threads — see lesson 6.

*** A Note on ~:Gender:~

The novel templates and the character capture no longer include a ~:Gender:~
property. Pronouns and presentation are part of who a character is, and the
profile has better places for them than a one-word field — the *Concept* line,
or the *Backstory*. Nothing in org-scribe reads the property, so adding it
back by hand is harmless but does nothing.

(Short story projects are the exception: their consolidated ~notes.org~ still
ships a character stub with ~:AGE:~ and ~:GENDER:~. The short story templates
have not been moved to the method's profile shape.)

** The Causal Chain

The seven lines at the top of the body are not a list of traits. They are a
chain, and *each one produces the next*:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Ghost  →  Lie  →  Weakness  →  Desire
#+END_EXAMPLE

Answer them in order. If two of them don't fit together, the wrong one is
usually the one *above* — a Lie that doesn't follow from the Ghost means you
have the wrong Ghost, not the wrong Lie.

*** Concept

Who they are and what they do, in one sentence, with a voice. How they would
describe themselves.

- Example: "A designer who has never once finished anything before the night
  it was due, and has decided that this is a personality."

*** Stance

Their position on the Theme, in their words. The same value as the ~:Stance:~
property.

- Example: "Being reliable is for people with nothing better to offer."

*** Ghost

What happened before the story starts and still hurts. *A concrete event, not
a climate* — "my grandmother died two years ago and I did not visit that last
month" rather than "an unhappy childhood".

The test is whether you could film it.

*** Lie

What they concluded from the Ghost, still believe, and is *not true*.

- Example: "If I commit to something properly and still fail, that's proof
  I'm worthless. Better not to commit properly."

The Lie is where the character's whole psychology hangs from. It has to be
false — a true conclusion is a lesson, not a Lie, and produces no arc.

*** Trouble

Something immediate complicating their life, that stops them sitting still.

*Theirs alone.* The World Problem in ~design.org~ is everyone's; the Trouble
is this person's. It exists so that the story can start without waiting for
the plot to arrive.

- Example: "Six months behind on rent, and the client who might fix that is
  the one they missed the deadline for."

*** Psychological Weakness

The internal defect that holds them back — a consequence of the Lie.

- Example: "Avoids anything that could be measured."

*** Moral Weakness

*Who they harm because of it, and how. One person, one act.*

This is the one writers skip, and it is the one that matters most. Note the
difference the template draws: being too ambitious holds *you* back — that is
psychological. Your children never seeing you is the *moral* harm. A character
with only a psychological weakness is sympathetic but inert; a character who
costs somebody else something has an arc worth watching.

- Example: "Sam covers for them, repeatedly, and Alex lets them — knowing what
  it costs Sam's own standing."

** Desire and Need

Two blocks with the same three parts, asked outward and then inward. The
template gives you the sentence to fill:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Wants (Goal), because they believe it will get them (Motivation), but
(Conflict) stops them.
#+END_QUOTE

*** Desire — Outward

*Goal*: concrete and external. The reader has to know when it has been achieved
and when it has not. "Being happy" is not a Goal; "stealing King Urdano's
treasure", "Get the Director's position", or "Buy the most expensive car" are.

*Motivation*: what they believe achieving it will give them. It comes out of
the Lie, so it is always false underneath. **If what you write here is healthy
and true, you have written the Need in the wrong box.**

*Conflict*: who or what stands in the way. External, and almost always the
opponent. **If the Conflict has no face, you do not have an opponent yet.**

*** Need — Inward

The same three parts, turned inward:

*Goal*: a change of behavior, not an object.

*Motivation*: what the Lie prevents them having. It usually names the harm in
the Moral Weakness.

*Conflict*: no longer the opponent, but *the Lie itself*, and what they would
have to admit about the Ghost to let it go. **If the Conflict here is the
opponent again, the arc is externalized** — nothing that happens inside the
character will depend on the character, and the ending will feel like something
that was done to them.

*** Example

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Desire

- Goal :: Land the Kestrel account and keep it for a full year.
- Motivation :: Then nobody gets to say they can't be relied on.
- Conflict :: Marcus, who holds the account and remembers the missed deadline.

,** Need

- Goal :: Ask for help before the thing is already on fire.
- Motivation :: To stop spending Sam's credibility instead of building their own.
- Conflict :: Admitting that the missed month with their grandmother was a
  choice, not a scheduling accident.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Notice the Desire's motivation is *false* ("then nobody gets to say...") and
the Need's Conflict is the Ghost, not Marcus. That is what the two boxes are
for.

** Plan

- *Plan*: how they mean to get the Desire. **It has to fail** — the opponent
  is strong, and a plan that works is a story that ends in chapter three.
- *Fallback plan*: more desperate and less clean. This is usually where the
  Moral Weakness gets its scene.

** Aspects

Short, double-edged sentences in the character's own voice.

The rule the template sets: *an Aspect is true, and it could equally save them
or sink them.* If it only does one of the two, it is not an Aspect yet — it is
a strength or a flaw, and those are less useful because the story can only
spend them once.

- Three at most for the protagonist, two for anyone else. More turns the
  character into a caricature.
- *Relationship*: an Aspect defining their bond with another character, group
  or organization.
- *Possession*: a significant object, if any — usually also their symbol.

*Example*:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
- Aspect :: I can talk anyone into giving me one more week.
- Aspect :: I notice exactly what people are ashamed of.
- Relationship :: Sam has never once let me fail in public.
- Possession :: My grandmother's watch, which I have never had repaired.
#+END_EXAMPLE

Each cuts both ways: talking your way into another week is charm and evasion;
noticing shame is empathy and a weapon.

** Backstory

A short scene where you *see* who they are, instead of saying what they are.

For you, not for the book — it does not have to be good and nobody will read
it. A good one shows the Ghost happening, or the character facing a moral
dilemma and resolving it according to their Lie.

** Interrogation

Five questions, one paragraph each. (In the shipped ~characters.org~; add it
by hand to a captured character.)

1. *What would they never do?* — the climax should threaten to force them to it.
2. *What do they do when they are alone and nobody is watching?* — that is the
   true self.
3. *What are they wrong about?* — the Lie in action.
4. *What would they die for?* — the moral core.
5. *Write them a trivial scene.* — ordering a drink, dealing with a minor
   annoyance. If it comes out generic, you do not know them yet.

Question 5 is the honest test of the whole profile.

** Change Milestones

A table, replacing the old free-text "character arc":

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
| Plot point       | Type       | What changes                          |
|------------------+------------+---------------------------------------|
| Midpoint         | Adjustment | Stops covering the missed deadline    |
| Apparent Defeat  | Break      | Admits the month with the grandmother |
| Moral Decision   | Break      | Refuses the account to protect Sam    |
#+END_EXAMPLE

Two rules make this more useful than a prose arc:

1. *Every milestone is anchored to a plot point* — one of the thirteen
   (Lesson 6). **If you cannot say in which scene it happens, it is not a
   milestone: it is a wish.** This is the sentence that catches the arc that
   exists only in the writer's head.
2. *Type is either Adjustment or Break.* An Adjustment is learning or gaining
   something, and a secondary Aspect shifts. A Break is changing *Stance on
   the Theme* — and those are rare, load-bearing, and should be countable on
   one hand.

Design the sequence backwards, from the final transformation.

** Relationships

There is no free-text "Relationships" section in the profile — relationships
are tracked structurally in the ~:RelationshipsData:~ property, managed by the
relationship commands covered later in this lesson. They give you a per-
character list and a whole-project table, and they survive renaming, which
free text does not.

What still belongs in prose is a relationship that is really an *Aspect* —
"Sam has never once let me fail in public" — which goes in the Aspects section
above.

** Notes Section

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Notes
-
#+END_EXAMPLE

Your personal notes:
- Voice and dialogue style
- Things to remember
- Revision notes
- Ideas for development

*Example*:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
- Internal monologue: Self-deprecating, slightly sarcastic, observant
- Dialogue: Honest, sometimes flustered, uses humor as defense
- Remember: Alex says "close enough" about everything (catchphrase)
- TODO: Develop their relationship with their parents more
#+END_EXAMPLE

* Filling Out Your Character Profile

You don't have to fill everything immediately!

** Minimum Profile (5 minutes)

Essential information:
- Name (heading)
- Role, Age, Gender (properties - already filled!)
- Main traits (3-5 adjectives)
- Desire (what they want)
- Initial state and final state (character arc endpoints)

This is enough to start writing!

** Full Profile (20-30 minutes)

Complete all sections when:
- Planning before drafting
- Developing complex characters
- During revision (understanding characters better)
- For main characters only (supporting cast can have minimal profiles)

** Practical Approach

1. *Before writing*: Minimum profile
2. *While writing*: Add details as they emerge
3. *During revision*: Fill in gaps, ensure consistency

The profile grows with your understanding of the character!

* The ID-Based Linking System

Now for the really cool part - linking characters to scenes!

** What is ID-Based Linking?

Remember that ~:ID:~ property? Here's what it enables:

*Traditional approach*:
- Type character name in ~:PoV:~ property
- Type character names in ~:Characters:~ property
- Hope you spelled consistently
- Manual searching

*ID-based linking*:
- Select character from a menu
- System inserts ID link automatically
- Click the link to jump to character profile
- Searches work even if you rename the character
- Character timeline generation

** How Links Look

In your scene properties, instead of plain text:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:PoV: Alex Rivera
:Characters: Alex Rivera, Sam Chen
#+END_EXAMPLE

You get clickable ID links:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:PoV: [[id:char-alex-rivera-001][Alex Rivera]]
:Characters: [[id:char-alex-rivera-001][Alex Rivera]], [[id:char-sam-chen-002][Sam Chen]]
#+END_EXAMPLE

*What you see in the file*: ~[[id:...][Alex Rivera]]~
*What you see in column view*: Just "Alex Rivera" (clean!)

** Benefits of ID Links

1. *Click to navigate*: Press ~C-c C-o~ on a character name → jump to their profile
2. *Return easily*: Press ~C-c &~ to come back
3. *Rename-safe*: Change "Alex" to "Alexander" in profile, all links still work
4. *Smart searches*: Find all scenes with a character, even if link format
5. *Timeline generation*: Automatic character appearance tracking
6. *Consistency*: Can't misspell if you're clicking from a menu!

* Setting PoV Characters (The Easy Way)

Instead of typing character names, use the menu system!

** Step 1: Position at Your Scene

Open your manuscript (story.org or novel.org) and put cursor on a scene heading.

** Step 2: Set PoV Character

From the main Hydra menu, press ~p~ ("set PoV") directly — this is a top-level Tag Scene action, not a submenu:

1. Press ~F8 F8~ (Hydra menu)
2. Press ~p~ (Set PoV character)

You'll see a completion menu with all your characters:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Set PoV character: [Alex Rivera] [Sam Chen] [Jordan Lee]
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Step 3: Select Character

Start typing the name (e.g., "Alex") and press Tab or Enter to select.

*Magic!* The ~:PoV:~ property is now:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:PoV: [[id:char-alex-rivera-001][Alex Rivera]]
#+END_EXAMPLE

Done! No typing, no typos, automatic linking!

* Setting Scene Characters (Multiple Characters)

For the ~:Characters:~ property (who appears in the scene):

** Step 1: Set Scene Characters

From the main Hydra menu, press ~h~ ("set characters") directly — this is a top-level Tag Scene action, not a submenu.

** Step 2: Multi-Select Characters

You'll see the same character list, but now you can select multiple:

1. Type/select first character (e.g., "Alex"), press Enter
2. Type/select second character (e.g., "Sam"), press Enter
3. Type/select third character if needed, press Enter
4. Press Enter on empty prompt to finish

** Step 3: Result

The ~:Characters:~ property now has comma-separated ID links:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:Characters: [[id:char-alex-rivera-001][Alex Rivera]], [[id:char-sam-chen-002][Sam Chen]]
#+END_EXAMPLE

*Beautiful!* All characters linked with one command.

* Jumping to Character Profiles

Need to check a character detail while writing?

** From Scene Property

1. Put cursor on a character name in ~:PoV:~ or ~:Characters:~
2. Press ~C-c C-o~ (org-open-at-point)
3. *Boom!* You're at the character profile

** Using the Menu

1. Cursor on scene heading
2. ~F8 F8 j~ (jump to PoV character — a top-level Tag Scene action)
3. *Boom!* At the PoV character's profile

** Return to Scene

After checking the character info:
1. Press ~C-c &~ (org-mark-ring-goto)
2. You're back at your scene!

*Workflow*: Writing scene → need to check eye color → ~C-c C-o~ → see profile → ~C-c &~ → back to writing. Takes 3 seconds!

* Character Timeline Visualization

See which characters appear in which scenes at a glance!

** What is a Character Timeline?

A table showing:
- Rows: Your scenes (in story order)
- Columns: Your characters
- Symbols: ◆ (PoV character) or ● (appears in scene)

Example:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
| Scene                  | Chapter   | Alex | Sam | Jordan |
|------------------------+-----------+------+-----+--------|
| Scene 1 - Alarm        | Chapter 1 | ◆    |     |        |
| Scene 2 - Coffee Shop  | Chapter 1 | ◆    | ●   |        |
| Scene 3 - Work Meeting | Chapter 1 | ●    |     | ●      |
| Scene 4 - Evening      | Chapter 2 | ◆    | ●   |        |
#+END_EXAMPLE

Reading the table:
- ◆ = This character is the PoV
- ● = This character appears (but isn't PoV)
- (blank) = Character doesn't appear

** Generating Character Timeline

*** Step 1: Find the Timeline Block

*Short story*: In ~story.org~, scroll to "Character Timeline" section
*Novel*: In ~novel.org~, scroll to "Progress Tracking" or "Character Timeline" section

You'll see:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,** Character Timeline

#+BEGIN: character-timeline
#+END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

*** Step 2: Generate Timeline

1. Put cursor on the ~#+BEGIN: character-timeline~ line
2. Press ~C-c C-c~
3. The table generates automatically!

** Using the Timeline

*What to look for*:

1. *Character coverage*: Do main characters appear regularly?
2. *PoV distribution*: Is PoV balanced (if multi-PoV)?
3. *Forgotten characters*: Any character with only one ●?
4. *Gap detection*: Long stretches without a character?
5. *Relationship tracking*: When do two characters share scenes?

*Example insights*:
- "Jordan only appears in one scene - should I cut them or develop more?"
- "Alex hasn't been PoV in 5 scenes - time to shift perspective!"
- "Sam appears in every scene - am I overusing this character?"

* Character Relationships (Structured Tracking)

** What are Structured Character Relationships?

Beyond the free-form "Relationships" section in character profiles, org-scribe provides a powerful system for tracking structured relationship data between characters.

Each relationship includes:
- *Target character*: linked by ID, so it stays valid across renaming
- *Type*: mentor, rival, friend, family, lover, enemy, ally, etc.

This structured data enables:
- Interactive relationship management
- A plain-list view per character and a project-wide table view

** Quick Setup

For existing projects with characters:
1. ~F8 F8 L r~ (or ~M-x org-scribe-setup-character-relationships~)
2. Adds ~:RelationshipsData:~ property to all characters
3. Ready to add relationships!

** Adding a Relationship

~F8 F8 L a~ (or ~M-x org-scribe-add-relationship~)

*Interactive prompts guide you*:

1. *From character*: "Alice Rivera"
2. *To character*: "Sam Chen"
3. *Relationship type*: "mentor" (from a list of common types, or type your own)

*Result*: "Added mentor relationship: Alice Rivera → Sam Chen"

The relationship is stored in Alice's =:RelationshipsData:= property:
#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
:RelationshipsData: [[id:char-sam-002][Sam Chen]]|mentor
#+END_EXAMPLE

** Common Relationship Types

The system suggests these common types (but you can use any text):

- =mentor/mentee= - Teaching/learning
- =friend= - Friendship
- =rival/opponent= - Competition
- =family= - Family connections (parent/child/sibling)
- =lover/spouse= - Romance
- =ally= - Working together
- =enemy= - Opposition
- =colleague/partner= - Professional
- =superior/subordinate= - Hierarchy

** Viewing Relationships

*** Single Character (~F8 F8 L v~)

View one character's relationships as a plain list:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
Relationships for Alice Rivera:

  - mentor: Sam Chen
  - rival: Jordan Lee
  - friend: Dana Park
#+END_EXAMPLE

*** All Relationships (~F8 F8 L V~)

View complete relationship table for the entire project:

| Character    | Related To   | Type   |
|--------------+--------------+--------|
| Alice Rivera | Sam Chen     | mentor |
| Alice Rivera | Jordan Lee   | rival  |
| Sam Chen     | Alice Rivera | mentee |
| Jordan Lee   | Alice Rivera | rival  |

Note: there is no dynamic-block/graph-visualization feature for relationships (no Graphviz/dot, ASCII-tree, or filterable network diagrams) — the two views above (a single character's list and the project-wide table) are the only display options. Relationships also no longer track a strength (1-5) or sentiment (positive/negative/neutral) value, only a directed source → target → type triple; if you want to note intensity or valence, put it in the relationship *type* itself (e.g. "close-ally" vs "ally") or in prose elsewhere on the character's heading.

** Practical Use Cases

*** Character Network Analysis

*Identify isolated characters*: scan ~F8 F8 L V~'s table for characters who never appear as either side of a relationship.

*Find central characters*: characters appearing in many rows (as source or target) are your most-connected — check whether your protagonist/antagonist are sufficiently connected.

*** Story Consistency

*Check relationship symmetry*: does Alice's entry list Sam as "mentor"? Does Sam's own entry list Alice back as "mentee"? Since each side stores its own relationship independently, it's easy for them to drift out of sync — the all-relationships table is the fastest way to spot a one-sided or contradictory pair.

*** Planning Relationship Evolution

Use relationships to track how connections change over the story:

1. Add a relationship when two characters first connect (~F8 F8 L a~)
2. Remove it (~F8 F8 L x~) and re-add it with a new type if the relationship changes kind (e.g. "acquaintance" → "friend" → "mentor")
3. Remove it entirely if it ends

** Removing Relationships

~F8 F8 L x~ (or ~M-x org-scribe-remove-relationship~)

1. Select character to modify
2. Choose which relationship to remove from the list
3. Confirmed!

** Relationship Data in Character Properties

Relationships are stored in the ~:RelationshipsData:~ property using this format:

#+BEGIN_EXAMPLE
,* Alice Rivera
:PROPERTIES:
:ID: char-alice-001
:RelationshipsData: [[id:char-sam-002][Sam]]|mentor; [[id:char-jordan-003][Jordan]]|rival
:END:
#+END_EXAMPLE

*Format*: ~[[id:TARGET-ID][Name]]|type~

- Uses ID links (stable across renames!)
- Semicolon-separated for multiple relationships
- Human-readable
- Manually editable if needed

** During Planning (Before Writing)

4. Update character profile with new insights
5. Generate character timeline
6. Look for gaps or overuse
7. Ensure all main characters appear regularly

* Common Questions

** "Do I need profiles for every character?"

*No!* Priority levels:

- *Protagonist*: full profile
- *Opponent*: full profile — it carries half the argument
- *Ally*: intermediate (no Ghost, Need or Plan)
- *Fake-Ally Opponent* and *Thematic Supporting*: reduced (Concept, Stance,
  Trouble, one Aspect, Desire)
- *Background characters*: no profile needed (waiter, clerk, etc.)

** "Can I link plain text character names later?"

*Yes!* The package includes batch conversion:

- ~F8 F8 R~ (relink project) links all scenes automatically
- Scans your manuscript for character names
- Converts plain text to ID links
- One command, entire manuscript updated

** "What if I change a character's name?"

*Easy!*

1. Go to character profile
2. Change the heading (the name)
3. ID links still work (they use the ID, not the name!)
4. Press ~F8 F8 R~ (relink project) to update the links.
5. All scenes will show now the new name

*No need to update each scene manually!*

** "Can I have multiple PoV characters?"

*Yes!* Common patterns:

- *Single PoV*: One character's perspective throughout
- *Multi-PoV rotating*: Different PoV each scene/chapter
- *Dual PoV*: Two main perspectives
- *Ensemble*: Many PoV characters

Just set ~:PoV:~ to whoever has the perspective in each scene.

** "How do I handle characters without names yet?"

*Strategies*:

1. Use placeholder: "Mysterious Stranger", "The Detective"
2. Use role: "Opponent", "Fake-Ally Opponent"
3. Use temporary name: "Bob" (change later)
4. Create profile when you know the name

The linking system works regardless!

** "My character list in the menu is empty!"

*Causes*:
1. No characters captured yet (create one!)
2. Characters lack IDs (run ~F8 F8 R~ to add IDs)
3. Wrong file open (must be in writing project)

*Quick fix*: ~F8 F8 L c~ (setup linking system)

* Troubleshooting

** "Character capture puts character in wrong file"

*Check*:
1. Are you in a writing project? (look for .writing-project file)
2. Is project type detected correctly?
3. For short stories: Does notes.org exist?
4. For novels: Does objects/ directory exist?

*Solution*: Make sure you're in the project directory when capturing.

** "Can't select character from menu - list is empty"

*Causes*:
1. Characters don't have IDs
2. Characters file not formatted correctly

*Solution*:
1. ~F8 F8 R~ (add IDs to all characters)
2. Make sure characters have ~:Role:~ or ~:TYPE:~ property

** "Link doesn't jump to character profile"

*Check*:
1. Does character profile have ~:ID:~ property?
2. Is ~:ID:~ value unique?
3. Is cursor directly on the link text?

*Solution*:
1. Click directly on character name in the link
2. Or use ~F8 F8 j~ to jump to PoV character

** "Character timeline shows no characters"

*Causes*:
1. Scenes don't have ~:PoV:~ or ~:Characters:~ properties set
2. Properties have plain text instead of ID links
3. Need to update the dynamic block

*Solution*:
1. Set character properties in scenes (~F8 F8 p~ and ~F8 F8 h~)
2. Update table (~C-c C-c~ on #+BEGIN line)

** "Character profile template is incomplete"

*If capture template looks wrong*:

1. Make sure org-scribe is up to date
2. Try ~M-x org-scribe-capture-character~ directly
3. Check that capture finalized (~C-c C-c~)

** Profile Depth Guide

The template itself says which parts each role can drop:

| Role                     | Profile      | Drop                                     |
|--------------------------+--------------+------------------------------------------|
| Protagonist              | Full         | nothing                                  |
| Opponent                 | Full         | nothing                                  |
| Ally                     | Intermediate | Ghost, Need, Plan                        |
| Fake-Ally Opponent       | Reduced      | all but Concept, Stance, Trouble, 1 Aspect, Desire |
| Thematic Supporting      | Reduced      | as above                                 |

The protagonist and the opponent get everything because the book's argument
runs between them. A walk-on needs no profile at all.

* Tips for Character Success

1. *Start minimal* - Don't spend hours on profiles before writing
2. *Develop while writing* - Add details as characters reveal themselves
3. *Get the chain right* - Ghost → Lie → Weakness → Desire is the minimum for a
   character who can carry an arc
4. *Link always* - Use the menu (~F8 F8 p~) instead of typing names
5. *Check consistency* - Jump to profiles when describing characters
6. *Track appearances* - Use timeline to ensure balanced screen time
7. *Revise profiles* - Update after drafting when you know characters better
8. *Distinguish voices* - Use Notes section for speech patterns/catchphrases
9. *Track relationships* - Use structured relationships (~F8 F8 L a~) to maintain character connections
10. *Review the table* - Use ~F8 F8 L V~ periodically to spot isolated or over-connected characters
11. *Check symmetry* - Make sure both sides of a relationship agree with each other
12. *Search often* - Review all scenes for a character during revision

** Remember

*Your characters are your story's heart.* The time you invest in understanding them - even with minimal profiles - will make your story richer, more consistent, and more emotionally resonant!
